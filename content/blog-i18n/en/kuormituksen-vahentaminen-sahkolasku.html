  • Stage the measures in three tiers: free scheduling changes, small control investments and only then larger purchases.
  • You can set timers yourself, but electrical installation work and changes to the grid connection are for an electrical contractor registered with Tukes (the Finnish Safety and Chemicals Agency) and for the network operator.

Reducing peak load means smoothing out peaks in electricity consumption: the same work gets done, but high-power equipment does not all run at once. This guide shows you how to identify peaks in your own consumption data and stage the measures by cost impact and effort, whether it is a warehouse, a workshop or a depot building.

What does reducing peak load mean in practice?

Reducing peak load does not mean driving less or heating the building less. The aim is to smooth out the peaks: to move consumption away from the hours when several high-power appliances run at the same time and push up the demand charge.

Energy and power are different things

Energy (kWh) is what you consume in total. Power (kW) is how much electricity is being used at any one moment. When heating, the compressor, the pressure washer and chargers all run at once, power rises and the meter records a peak. Think of a water supply: energy is the water used over the whole day, power is how many taps are open at the same time. If every tap is opened at once, the flow rate goes up, even though the day's total volume of water stays the same.

Why the peak, not total consumption, is what counts

Not all network tariffs have a demand charge: small connections often pay only a standing charge and an energy-based distribution charge. In demand-based tariffs, used particularly for larger business connections, part of the charge is based on the measured peak power. The basis of calculation varies between network operators: for example, Elenia's price list for large customers uses the month's highest 60-minute average power, while Kuopion Sähköverkko's low-voltage demand tariff uses the highest average hourly power over the past 12 months. In the latter case, a single unsmoothed peak shows up on the bill for a whole year.

The same idea works in the day-to-day running of a haulage firm: once you can identify the rush peaks, you can stagger the work. More on that at the end of the article.

How do you identify load peaks from your own consumption data?

Peaks are found in your own metering data, not by guesswork. You need hourly or 15-minute consumption data for 12 months, a spreadsheet program and an hour of your time.

Step 1: download consumption data for the past 12 months

In Finland, electricity metering data are stored in Datahub, run by the transmission system operator Fingrid. Log in to the Datahub customer portal (oma.datahub.fi) with Suomi.fi identification (the Finnish public e-identification service), or use the online service of your own network operator, such as Caruna or Elenia, and download the data as a CSV file. Note that times in the Datahub file are in UTC. If the building has its own sub-meter, its data are even more precise.

A full year covers both the winter heating peaks and summer cooling. Check that the timestamps in the file are hourly or 15-minute and that as little data as possible is missing from the period.

Step 2: work out the peak hour and the average power

Sort the data in descending order. The largest single hour is your peak, and the hours around it tell you which appliances start up at the same time. Then calculate the average power for the year. The ratio of peak to average power tells you how much room there is for smoothing.

If, for example, your peak is 25 kW and your average power is 8 kW, the ratio is over three. That means the peak comes from a few hours in which many appliances run at the same time. If you stagger them, the peak can fall noticeably, and the change in the demand charge can be calculated directly.

Step 3: mark the peaks on a calendar and look for recurring patterns

Record when each peak occurs: date, time and day of the week. Recurring patterns matter more than individual peaks, because routines can be changed.

A single peak is rarely a problem. If the peak hour falls every weekday between 15:00 and 17:00, when the wash bay's water heater, the compressor and the chargers all start at once, it is not chance but structure. A routine like that is the easiest to fix: move one appliance an hour later and measure again. Once the peaks are marked on a calendar, a staged plan comes together quickly.

The big electricity users in a small warehouse, workshop or depot are usually easy to identify. A power peak is typically caused by a few appliances that start up at the same time.

Heating and ventilation behind winter peaks

In the Nordic countries, heating is usually the largest adjustable load. Lower the building temperature at night and at weekends, and move the ventilation boost away from the morning peak. Note that if the whole building is quickly heated back up to working temperature in the morning, the heating itself can become a new peak, so start heating early or in stages. If the heat pump runs at full power at 7:00, bring its start forward to 5:00–6:00, when the rest of the load is still low.

Machines and tools behind daytime peaks

The compressor's start-up spike often coincides with heating. The compressor runs hardest when the tank pressure drops and the machine refills it. If several mechanics start using air tools at 8:00, the compressor and the heating push up the peak together. A timed fill before the shift starts moves this spike away from the other appliances' peak.

Charging electric vehicles and machinery

Schedule charging for the night, when other consumption is low. With several charging points, stagger the start times so that the first vehicle starts charging at 22:00, the second at 23:00 and the third at midnight, or use load management that shares the available power between the charging points. Many chargers already support scheduling, so no investment may be needed.

How do you prioritise the measures?

Stage the measures in three tiers: free scheduling changes first, then small control investments and only then larger purchases. Move to the next tier only once the previous one has been done and measured; otherwise you will buy an expensive solution to a problem that a free fix would have solved.

Tier 1: move consumption away from peak hours

Start by scheduling everything that can be done for free or cheaply. The start times of water heaters, dryers and chargers can often be moved using the appliances' own timers. Forklift and machinery charging should be scheduled for the night. Record the measured peak before and after, so you know how much tier 1 achieved. Changing the control of a permanently installed appliance, such as a water heater, is however electrical work that must be done by an electrical contractor.

Tier 2: control and automation

When scheduling is no longer enough, add control. Motion sensors, staggered thermostats and remote-controlled sockets cost tens or hundreds of euros and remove the risk of forgetting – the same idea as in dispatch: whatever repeats every day is worth automating. At the same time, make sure you can see consumption at hourly or 15-minute level, so the effect of each measure can be verified. A plug-in smart socket is only suitable for appliances whose power is within the socket's rating; high-power appliances are controlled with a permanently installed control system.

Tier 3: investments that only pay off if the peak stays high

Larger purchases, such as replacing the heating system or a load management system for charging, only pay off once tiers 1 and 2 have been done and the peak is still high. First work out how many kilowatts the investment would take off the peak, and compare that with the cost of the investment.

An example: tiers 1 and 2 reduced the peak from 20 kW to 12 kW, and a new heating solution would cost €8,000. If it took a further 2 kW off the peak and the demand charge is €3 per kilowatt per month, the saving is €6 a month and the payback period is over a hundred years. So the investment does not pay off because of the demand charge; it can only pay off if it also saves energy.

How does reducing peak load show up on the electricity bill?

The demand charge on the network bill

If your network tariff includes a demand charge, smoothing out the peaks shows up in the demand charge on your network bill. Electricity tax and energy-based charges depend on the kilowatt-hours consumed, so staggering does not affect them. When the saving shows up depends on how the demand charge is calculated: if it is based on the month's highest power, the saving appears on the next bill, but if it is based on the highest power over the past 12 months, the old peak will continue to affect the bill for months. Changes to the connection's main fuses or connection capacity are agreed with the network operator under its connection terms.

How to calculate the saving yourself

Record the peak power (kW) used as the billing basis before and after the changes. Multiply the reduction in power by the demand charge unit price, which you will find in your network operator's price list, and the comparison is done. An example: if peak power falls from 18 kW to 12 kW and the demand charge is €3 per kilowatt per month, the saving is €18 a month, or €216 a year. If the tier 1 and tier 2 measures cost €150, they pay for themselves in about eight months.

When do you need the network operator or an electrical contractor?

Most peak-load reduction can be done yourself: appliance operating times are moved, heating is controlled with existing settings and consumption is tracked from the metering data. You can change when appliances are used, but you cannot carry out electrical installation work.

A professional is needed when it comes to connection capacity, main fuses, metering, the distribution board or fixed installations. If, for example, charging electric lorries requires more power than the existing connection allows, a change to the connection is always agreed with the network operator. Under the Finnish Electrical Safety Act (1135/2016), electrical work may only be carried out by a business that has registered with Tukes and has a qualified electrical work supervisor; a layperson may only do very simple jobs. More information is available on the Tukes website.

The practical dividing line is simple: if the work concerns the connection or the building's electrical installations, contact an electrical contractor. If it concerns when an appliance starts up using its own settings, you can do it yourself and record the result. For example, scheduling a plug-in appliance with a smart socket is your own measure, but upgrading the main fuses from, say, 25 amps to 35 amps so that more charging points can run at once requires a decision on the connection by the network operator and work by an electrical contractor.

How can the same peak management be applied more widely in a haulage firm?

Peak hours in dispatch too

The same measure–identify–stagger model works for the work itself. Record what time of day the phone rings most: typically in the morning before departures and in the afternoon before planning the next day. These are your firm's load peaks, and they take time away from actual dispatch and invoicing.

Once the peaks are on paper, you can stage the measures: first the recurring questions (sign-off, transport order, payment date), then finding loads in one go, and finally the invoicing chain. If, for example, 60 % of calls come between 7:00 and 9:00 and between 14:00 and 16:00, move routine tasks out of those hours and ask for the sign-off as soon as the load has arrived, not by phone the next morning.

RAHTIS on the same principle

In RAHTIS, the customer can see the stages of the job and the estimated time of arrival via the tracking link, so there are fewer "where's the lorry?" calls. The driver signs off the delivery in the app, an invoice to your own customer is created from the job and payment status is tracked in the same place: https://www.rahtis.eu/en.

In a small firm where you handle dispatch, invoicing and safety yourself, smoothing out workload peaks means the same as with electricity: less overlapping load in the busiest hours, and a measurable result. Do the heaviest work when the grid or your calendar has slack, not when everyone else is trying to do the same.

FAQ

How much is the demand charge in Finland, and where can I see it?

The price and the basis of calculation vary between network operators, so there is no general figure. Check your network contract or your network operator's price list to see whether your tariff has a demand charge, its unit price in euros per kilowatt per month and how the billed power is determined. For example, in the 2026 price lists the demand charge for low-voltage demand tariffs was about €1.3/kW/month at Kuopion Sähköverkko and about €3/kW/month for Elenia's large-customer demand tariff.

Can I identify the peaks myself, or do I need a separate meter?

You can identify them yourself. The metering data are in Datahub, and you can download them as a CSV file from the Datahub customer portal or your network operator's online service. A separate meter is not essential, but your own sub-meter gives a more precise picture of the load from individual appliances.

How long a period is needed to identify the peaks?

12 months is a suitable period. It covers both the winter heating peaks and the summer, so you see the variation over the whole year. A shorter period can be misleading if, for example, the peak only occurs on freezing mornings.

Will the connection charge go down if consumption is staggered?

Not directly. Staggering reduces the demand charge, if you have one, but the connection charge is determined by the size of the connection. Downsizing the connection and its effect on charges are agreed with the network operator under its connection terms.

Is it worth moving electric vehicle charging to the night if there are several charging points?

Yes, especially with several charging points. Several vehicles charging at once in the evening drive up power quickly, and depending on how the demand charge is calculated, a single peak hour can affect the bill for the whole month or even the whole year. Schedule charging for the night and stagger it so that the first vehicle starts charging at 22:00, the second at 23:00 and the third at midnight. Many chargers already support scheduling.
