People searching for "90 working hours" are often looking for a rule that does not exist: the Finnish Working Hours Act (872/2019) has no 90-hour flexibility over a four-month period. In road haulage, the figure 90 comes from the EU driving and rest time regulation, which caps driving time over two consecutive weeks at 90 hours. Working time, meanwhile, is limited by the 80-hour two-week period in the collective agreement for the truck sector, the drivers' 60-hour weekly cap and the 48-hour average. Below you will find a worked example and rostering guidance to keep you within the limits.

What does "90 working hours" mean in law?

There is no 90-hour rule in the Working Hours Act. The Working Hours Act 872/2019, in force since the start of 2020, replaced the old Working Hours Act 605/1996, and neither of them allows working time to be agreed to exceed the contractual hours by an average of 90 hours over a four-month period. In road haulage, 90 hours is a driving time limit: total driving time over two consecutive weeks may not exceed 90 hours (Regulation 561/2006, Article 6).

The provisions and their purpose

A driver's hours are limited by three different sets of rules. The driving and rest time regulation 561/2006 limits driving time: no more than 9 hours a day (10 hours twice a week), 56 hours a week and 90 hours over two consecutive weeks. The Working Hours Act 872/2019 limits total working time: working time including overtime may average no more than 48 hours a week over four months, and a driver within the scope of the driving and rest time regulation may also work no more than 60 hours in any calendar week (section 18). The collective agreement sets regular working time and overtime pay.

The limits exist for road safety and to keep drivers from burning out: the driving time limit prevents excessively long driving stints, and the working time limit stops loading, unloading and waiting from stretching the week unreasonably. Employer and driver cannot agree between themselves to depart from these limits. A national collective agreement may extend the reference period for the 48-hour average (Working Hours Act, section 34), and the collective agreement for the truck sector uses the calendar year as the reference period (section 11, point 2).

What can be agreed in the employment contract?

The driving time and maximum working time limits cannot be overridden by an employment contract. There is room for agreement in how regular working time is arranged: under the collective agreement for the truck sector, regular working time is 80 hours per two-week period (period-based working time, Working Hours Act section 7), and overtime always requires the driver's consent (Working Hours Act section 17). Write the applicable collective agreement, the regular working time and the pay basis into the employment contract, so that rostering and payroll are lawful.

Once the working time terms are correctly set out in the contract, it makes sense to track them in the same place as the jobs. RAHTIS is designed for small haulage firms where the owner does the planning: https://www.rahtis.eu/fi.

How does "90 working hours" differ from the "40" and "75" working time figures?

The three figures mean different things. 40 hours is regular weekly working time under the general working time rules: no more than 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week (Working Hours Act, section 5). 75 is not a statutory limit in road haulage; it usually refers to a 7.5-hour working day, i.e. a 37.5-hour week, which is regular working time under many office-sector collective agreements. 90 hours, on the other hand, is the driving time limit for two consecutive weeks under Regulation 561/2006, not a working time limit.

Why the figures get mixed up

The confusion arises because all three concern the same question: how much a driver may drive or work. In a small haulage firm, tachograph driving times, the two-week pay period and the weekly rota run side by side, and driving time is easily confused with working time.

A rule of thumb: compare driving time with 9, 56 and 90, and total working time with 80 (the two-week regular working time in the collective agreement), 60 (the weekly cap) and 48 (the average). A single week may run to, say, 55 hours, as long as the 60-hour weekly cap and the 48-hour average hold and hours above 80 in the two-week period are paid as overtime. In haulage, peaks come irregularly, and that is exactly why the two-week period is practical: hours from a busy week can be balanced out with a quieter one.

When does the 90-hour limit apply?

The 90-hour driving time limit applies whenever a transport operation falls within the scope of the driving and rest time regulation 561/2006: as a rule, goods transport in vehicles over 3.5 tonnes and, from 1 July 2026, international transport in vehicles over 2.5 tonnes as well. The limit applies to every period of two consecutive calendar weeks and cannot be overridden by agreement. It also applies to self-employed drivers, because the regulation limits driving, not employment.

What the employer must record

The employer must keep working time records of each driver's hours worked, overtime hours and the overtime pay made for them (Working Hours Act, section 32), and must give the driver a driver's logbook, for which a tachograph may be used instead (section 31). The work roster must be notified in writing no later than one week before the start of the period it covers (section 30).

In road haulage, the collective agreement changes the statutory starting points, so first check whether your firm applies the collective agreement for the truck sector (between the employers' federation ATL and the transport workers' union AKT). Even a one-lorry firm should put the employment contract in writing as soon as it hires: it should state the regular working time, the pay basis and the applicable collective agreement. Fix the two-week periods in advance, for example always starting on a Monday, and track the 48-hour average over whichever reference period applies to you. That way balancing out hours is based on a plan, not on random peaks.

How are hours calculated in a two-week period?

Worked example: a two-week period

Take the basic case: the driver is covered by the collective agreement for the truck sector, so regular working time is 80 hours per two-week period. In the first week the driver drives for 50 hours and does 8 hours of loading and other work, giving 58 hours of working time. In the second week driving may total no more than 40 hours, because driving time over the two weeks may not exceed 90 hours. The driver drives for 30 hours and does 6 hours of other work, 36 hours in total.

Working time for the period is 58 + 36 = 94 hours. Regular working time is exceeded by 14 hours: the first 12 are paid at +50 % and the next 2 at +100 %. Driving time is 50 + 30 = 80 hours, below the 90-hour limit, and each week stays under the 60-hour weekly cap. Driver B drives 56 + 34 hours and uses up the full 90-hour driving limit. In the first week, on top of 56 hours of driving, there is room for only 4 hours of other work before the 60-hour weekly cap is reached.

Overtime and shift allowances in the same calculation

Over a longer period, you check the average: 48 hours a week means no more than about 835 hours of working time, including overtime, over a four-month period (about 17.4 weeks). If a driver regularly works 94-hour two-week periods, the average is 47 hours a week, so the limit is close. All time spent working counts, including hours falling on a Sunday or at night.

Overtime and evening and night allowances do not change how the limits are calculated, but they are linked to it: hours above 80 in the two-week period are overtime paid at the higher rate, and evening and night allowances are paid separately under the collective agreement for the hours that qualify. So record three figures for every shift: driving time, other working time, and hours falling in the evening or at night. With these kept apart, checking the period is a matter of simple addition, not guesswork.

How does the 90-hour driving limit affect driver rostering?

Driving time limits and working time limits have to be planned together. Regulation 561/2006 limits daily driving time to nine hours (ten hours twice a week), weekly driving time to 56 hours and driving time over two consecutive weeks to 90 hours. In domestic distribution and terminal work, the working time limit is usually reached before the driving time limit, whereas on long international runs the driving time limit bites first.

Combining driving and rest times with the working time cap

A driver's actual working day is longer than the hours spent driving: loading, unloading, terminal work and maintenance all count as working time. When planning the week, add up driving and other work and compare the total with the 60-hour weekly cap and the 48-hour average, and compare driving time separately with the 56-hour and 90-hour limits. That way you can see which one constrains you first.

A 56-hour driving week is only possible if other work in the same week is no more than 4 hours, because a driver may work no more than 60 hours in a calendar week. Driving the following week is then limited to 34 hours. In domestic distribution, less driving time accrues, but terminal work and loading fill working time quickly, so the 60-hour weekly cap and the 48-hour average are reached before the driving time limits. The planner's job is to know which limit binds first and to allocate shifts accordingly.

How dispatch tracks the running totals week by week

Keep the running totals visible every Monday: for each driver, driving time and working time in the two-week period, driving time remaining before the 90-hour limit, and the working time average since the start of the reference period. When driving time approaches 90 or weekly working time approaches 60, move jobs to a driver with spare capacity or shorten the days. That way you avoid breaching a limit purely through poor planning.

What are the most common mistakes in applying the 90-hour limit?

The most common mistake is to think that 90 hours is a working time flexibility that can be written into the employment contract. It is not: 90 hours is the two-week driving time limit, and the working time limits always apply on top of it. Work beyond regular working time is overtime or additional work, and it must be paid for.

The second mistake is counting only driving time: driving time can stay below 90 hours even though working time exceeds the 60-hour weekly cap. The third is calculating overtime pay wrongly, for example by paying all hours above 80 in the two-week period at +50 %, even though after 12 hours the rate is +100 %. The fourth is relying on a paper notebook: without working time records you cannot prove the running total, nor spot an error until the driver claims compensation.

How to put mistakes right afterwards

The fix is to recalculate the actual working time for the period, pay any missing overtime retrospectively and document the procedure for future periods. The Finnish occupational safety and health authorities require employers to keep reliable records of each employee's working time and to retain them so that the basis for pay can be verified. For drivers, tachograph data supplement the working time records but do not replace them.

If the error is only discovered after the period has ended, the unpaid overtime must be paid retrospectively. An example: a driver has been underpaid by 2 hours of overtime in every two-week period. There are 26 periods a year, so the missing overtime adds up to 52 hours per driver, and 624 hours in a firm with 12 drivers. A driver can claim the pay within two years of the end of the calendar year in which the right to it arose (Working Hours Act, section 40). A regular check every Monday is cheaper than a lump-sum payout after the event.

How does a working time system help you comply with driving and working time limits?

A working time system has three jobs: calculate working hours and overtime for the two-week period automatically, import driving times directly from the tachograph, and warn you before the 60-hour weekly cap, the 48-hour average or the 90-hour driving limit is reached. Tracking on paper or in Excel usually falls apart at the point, midway through the period, when you need to know how many hours each driver has left.

What RAHTIS covers and what it doesn't

RAHTIS brings dispatch, the freight exchange and driver management together in one place, so rostering and driver details stay in a single view. The driver report calculates hours from the recorded shifts and, when a pay model based on the collective agreement for the truck sector has been selected for the driver, overtime for the two-week period; the report can be exported as PDF, Excel or CSV. To be honest: RAHTIS does not read tachograph files and does not track the 90-hour driving limit, the 60-hour weekly cap or the 48-hour average. The report is an aid for internal payments, not payroll, so make sure statutory tracking comes from your tachograph software and your payroll.

Selection criteria for a small haulage firm

Ask for these three features before you buy: a per-driver report of two-week periods and the 48-hour average, import of tachograph data (vehicle unit and driver card) without manual re-keying, and an alert before a limit is reached, not after. If the system requires a separate licence per driver, ask for the total cost at the scale of 12 lorries before you commit.

FAQ

Can I use the "90 working hours" rule if it isn't mentioned in the employment contract?

No, because no such rule exists. The Working Hours Act does not allow working time to be agreed to exceed the contractual hours by 90 hours over a four-month period. Hours above regular working time are overtime, which requires the driver's consent and is paid at the higher rate, and total working time is limited by the 60-hour weekly cap and the 48-hour average. Check your sector's collective agreement first and write the regular working time into the employment contract as soon as you hire.

How many hours a month is "90 working hours"?

90 hours is a two-week driving time limit, not a monthly working time. Driving may total no more than 90 hours in two consecutive weeks and no more than 56 hours in a single week, so monthly driving time depends on how the weeks fall within the month. Working time is limited by the average: 48 hours a week is about 209 hours a month (48 × 4.35 weeks), including overtime. An individual month may differ, as long as the average over the reference period adds up and no week exceeds 60 hours.

Does a driver's driving time count as working time?

Yes, but driving time alone is not the whole of working time. Working time also includes loading, unloading, terminal work and maintenance, so hours driven are always fewer than total working time. Compare driving time with the 56-hour and 90-hour driving limits, and the sum of driving and other work with the 60-hour weekly cap and the 48-hour average. Whichever limit is reached first is the one that decides.

Does unused driving time carry over to the next period?

No. The 90-hour limit applies to every period of two consecutive weeks, so driving time left unused in a quiet week does not raise the limit for the following weeks, and weekly driving time is capped at 56 hours in any case. As for working time, hours above 80 in the two-week period are paid as overtime in that period's pay, and a short period cannot be offset against overtime in the next one.

Does the 90-hour limit also apply to subcontracted haulage?

Yes. The working time and rostering of a subcontractor's drivers are the subcontractor's responsibility, and the subcontractor answers for their lawfulness, but the 90-hour driving limit applies to every driver within the scope of the driving and rest time regulation, including subcontractors' drivers and self-employed drivers. A self-employed driver's working time is also limited by the Act on the Working Time of Self-Employed Drivers in Road Transport (349/2013). So before you start working together, make sure the schedules you give a partner can be driven within the limits.
