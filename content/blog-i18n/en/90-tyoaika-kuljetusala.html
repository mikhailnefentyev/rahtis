The "90 working hours" rule means that, on top of the working time set in the employment contract, work may add up to no more than 90 extra hours on average over a four-month period, under section 28 of the Finnish Working Hours Act. In driver rostering this limit acts as a ceiling, which the EU driving and rest time rules narrow further still. Below you will find a practical worked example and a rota calendar to keep you on the right side of the law.

What does the "90 working hours" rule mean in law?

The "90 working hours" rule is a flexibility arrangement agreed in the employment contract: working time can be agreed to be, on average, up to 90 hours longer than the working time set in the employment contract over a four-month period (Finnish Working Hours Act 8/1996, section 28). It is an average, not a fixed weekly working time.

The provision and its purpose

Section 28 of the Working Hours Act allows employer and employee to agree that average working time over a four-month period may exceed the contractual working time by up to 90 hours. The aim is to allow flexibility in seasonal or fluctuating work: more hours in the busy season, fewer in quieter times. The averaging reference period may be up to 52 weeks, but the four-month period is the most common yardstick.

The law therefore gives the employer no unilateral right to extend working time; it always requires an agreement between the two parties. This protects the employee where workloads vary unpredictably, and gives the employer predictability across the seasons. Without such an agreement the employer has to fall back on statutory overtime, which is more expensive and subject to stricter rules. The flexibility is thus a tool for keeping costs under control, provided both parties understand its terms and consequences.

When is the 90-hour addition open to agreement?

The addition always requires an agreement: either the employment contract or an applicable collective agreement that permits it. Without an agreement, working time cannot be extended on average. In haulage this means the driver's employment contract must state both the basic working time and the 90-hour average addition, so that rostering and payroll are lawful.

How does the "90" rule differ from the "40" and "75" working time figures?

The three figures mean different things. "40" refers to standard weekly working time: 40 hours a week, the starting point in most employment relationships. "75" may refer to the maximum for a shorter period, such as two weeks, or to limits agreed in sector agreements. "90", on the other hand, is an addition on top of the contractual working time: the average addition of up to 90 hours over a four-month period under section 28 of the Working Hours Act.

Why the figures get mixed up

The confusion arises because all three concern the same question: how much work may be done. In a small haulage firm, weekly rotas and the four-month average run side by side, and a driver's timesheets easily blur the weekly and period levels.

A rule of thumb: at weekly level you look at 40 hours; at period level you calculate the 90-hour addition as an average over four months. When planning shifts, check the week first and then make sure the four-month average stays within the limit. A single week may rise to 55 hours if hours are cut correspondingly the following week. That is precisely the flexibility the 90-hour rule exists for. In haulage, peaks come irregularly, and a fixed 40-hour week is often hard to deliver without either the employer or the driver losing out.

When does the "90 working hours" rule apply?

The 90-hour addition is an exception, not the default. It may only be used where the employment contract or a local agreement expressly states that regular working time may be extended by up to 90 hours per four-month period. Without such an agreement, working time remains as defined in the employment contract.

Agreement requirements and the duty to put it in writing

The agreement to use the 90 hours must be made in writing, and by the end of the period working time must be brought back to the contractual average. The rules on defining working time and on shift work are found in sections 9–11 of the Working Hours Act 8/1996.

In haulage, the collective agreement may change the starting points, so check your own sector's collective agreement (TES) first before writing the 90-hour clause into employment contracts. Even a one-lorry firm should do this as soon as it hires: without a written agreement the driver's overtime is calculated on a different basis, and sorting it out after the event is expensive. The clause should specify exactly which four-month period applies and how the average is calculated. Alternatively the period can be a calendar quarter, but it is usually defined by season: January–April, May–August, September–December. This ties the flexibility to the nature of the business rather than to random peaks.

How is the "90 working hours" rule calculated in shift work?

Worked example: 4 months, 12 lorries

Take the basic case: the weekly working time agreed in the employment contract is 40 hours. The 90-hour addition under section 28 of the Working Hours Act is spread over the four-month period, so the maximum average for the period is about 48.75 hours a week (360 h divided by 7.4 weeks, added to the 40 h base). In practice the check is done on the total hours for the period: 40 h/week times about 17.4 weeks is about 696 hours, and the maximum is about 866 hours.

A concrete example: driver A works 200 hours in January (peak season), 180 hours in February, 160 hours in March and 120 hours in April (quiet season). That totals 660 hours, below the 866-hour ceiling. The average is about 37.9 hours a week, i.e. below the 40-hour base. Driver B works 220 + 220 + 220 + 206 hours over the same period, a total of 866 hours. The average is exactly 48.75 hours a week, which is precisely the permitted maximum. The differences between the drivers come down to how shifts are allocated and to seasonal variation.

Overtime and shift allowances in the same calculation

Month by month, the period breaks down like this: 17.4 weeks divided over four months gives about 4.3 weeks a month. If the driver's rota is 44 hours a week, about 189 hours accrue per month, and the four-month total stays within the 866-hour ceiling. Every hour of shift work counts in full, including hours that fall on a Sunday or at night.

Overtime and shift allowances do not change the 90-hour calculation, but they are linked to it: hours above the 48.75-hour weekly average are overtime, paid at the overtime rate, and the shift allowance is paid separately for every hour falling within shift work. So record three separate figures for each shift: hours worked, hours falling within shift work, and overtime hours. With these kept apart, checking the four-month period is a matter of simple addition, not guesswork.

How does the "90 working hours" rule affect driver rostering?

In practice, the EU driving and rest time rules often limit working time before the 90-hour ceiling is reached. Regulation (EC) No 561/2006 limits daily driving time to nine hours and sets the weekly rest requirements, which means long domestic and terminal shifts fill the 90-hour ceiling faster than long international runs do.

Combining driving and rest times with the working time ceiling

A driver's actual working day is longer than the hours spent driving: loading, terminal work, maintenance and waiting all count as working time. When planning the week, add up both driving and other work and compare the total with both the 90-hour four-month average and the weekly driving time limit. That way you can see which one constrains you first.

In international driving, the weekly driving limit is 56 hours and the fortnightly limit is 90 hours, but loading, unloading and waiting are added to working time on top. In domestic distribution, less driving time accrues, but terminal work and loading fill working time quickly. This is why the 90-hour four-month average is often reached before the driving time limits. The planner's job is to know which rule binds first and to allocate shifts accordingly.

How dispatch tracks the running total week by week

Keep the period total visible every Monday: working time used per driver, hours remaining in the period, and a forecast of the average for the rest of the period. When the running total approaches 90, move shifts to a driver with spare capacity or shorten the days. That way you avoid overtime liabilities arising purely from poor planning.

What are the most common mistakes in applying the "90 working hours" rule?

The most common mistake is using the 90-hour addition when no flexibility has been agreed at all in the employment contract or a local agreement. In that case the extra hours are overtime to be paid, and they do not count towards the flexibility allowance.

The second mistake is forgetting to balance the period: if, at the end of the four-month period, average weekly working time exceeds the contractual figure, the difference is paid as overtime afterwards. The third is calculating the shift allowance wrongly, for example by not applying it to hours that the 90-hour flexibility has pushed into the evening or night. The fourth is relying on a paper notebook: without proper timesheets you cannot prove the running total, nor spot an error until the driver claims compensation.

How to put mistakes right afterwards

The fix is to recalculate the actual working time for the period, pay any outstanding amounts retrospectively and document the procedure for future periods. The Finnish occupational safety and health authority requires employers to keep reliable records of each employee's working time and to retain them so that the basis for pay can be verified. For drivers, driving and rest time data supplement the timesheets but do not replace them.

If the error is only discovered after the period has ended, the employer must pay the difference as overtime. This can come as a surprise: at the end of the four-month period it turns out the average has been exceeded by 2 hours a week, and over 17.4 weeks that is about 35 hours per driver. For an eleven-lorry firm that is nearly 400 hours of overtime pay that could have been avoided with weekly monitoring. A regular check every Monday is cheaper than a one-off payout four months later.

How does a working time system help you comply with the "90 working hours" rule?

A working time system has three jobs: track the build-up of the 90 extra hours automatically over the four-month period, import drivers' driving and rest times directly from the digital tachograph vehicle unit, and warn you before the ceiling is reached. Tracking on paper or in Excel usually falls apart at the point, midway through the period, when you need to know how many hours each driver has left.

What RAHTIS covers and what it doesn't

RAHTIS brings dispatch, the freight exchange and driver management together in one place, so rostering and driver details stay in a single view. To be honest: RAHTIS does not currently perform automatic 90-hour period tracking under section 28 of the Working Hours Act. That should come from a separate payroll or working time system that calculates the totals officially. Use RAHTIS for planning and recording allocations, but make sure the statutory tracking comes from the separate system's reports.

Selection criteria for a small haulage firm

Ask for these three features before you buy: an automatic four-month running total report per driver, import of digital tachograph (vehicle unit) data without manual re-keying, and an alert before the ceiling is reached, not after. If the system requires separate licences per driver, ask for the total cost at the scale of 12 lorries before you commit.

FAQ

Can I use the "90 working hours" rule if it isn't mentioned in the employment contract?

No. The flexibility always requires an express agreement, either in the employment contract or in the applicable collective agreement. Without a written clause, the extra hours count as overtime paid at the overtime rate and do not go towards the 90-hour allowance. Check your sector's collective agreement first, and if it permits it, still write the clause into the employment contract as soon as you hire.

How many hours a month is the "90 working hours" rule?

It depends on the basic working time in your employment contract. Taking a 40-hour week as an example: the maximum for a four-month period is about 866 hours; divided by about 4.3 weeks a month, that comes to roughly 190–217 hours a month. The average over the period may rise to no more than about 48.75 hours a week, but an individual month may differ as long as the average at the end of the period adds up.

Does a driver's driving time alone count as working time in the "90 working hours" calculation?

Not on its own. A driver's working time also includes loading, terminal work, maintenance and waiting, so hours driven are always fewer than total working time. Add up both driving and other work and compare the total with both the 90-hour four-month average and the limits under the EU driving and rest time rules. Whichever limit is reached first is the one that decides.

What happens if hours are left unused at the end of the four-month period?

Unused hours do not carry over to the next period, nor are they converted into compensation. The period is a closed unit: by the end of it, average working time must be brought into line with the agreement, and the new period starts from zero. If, on the other hand, the average exceeds the agreed figure, the difference is paid as overtime afterwards.

Should the "90 working hours" rule also be applied to subcontracted haulage?

No. The "90 working hours" rule only applies to your own employed drivers, because it is a flexibility based on the employment contract or collective agreement. The working time and rostering of a subcontractor's drivers are the subcontractor's responsibility, and the subcontractor answers for compliance. Even so, it is worth making sure the partner's contract terms and timesheets are in order before you start working together.
