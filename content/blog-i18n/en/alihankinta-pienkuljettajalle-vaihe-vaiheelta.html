In practice, subcontracting means finding freight on a freight exchange or from your own customers, quoting, completing the job and then invoicing with a POD attached. This guide covers the whole chain, from posting your availability to payment tracking, for a small firm of 1–12 lorries that does its own dispatching and invoicing. Subcontracting is the quickest way to fill a lorry when your own customer base is not enough, which is why managing it is a core skill for every owner-driver.

What exactly does subcontracting mean?

In road haulage, subcontracting means carrying out transport on behalf of another company: the client sells the job to its customer, and you do it with your own vehicle and driver. The client invoices the end customer; you invoice the client. The same model works the other way round, too, when you can't cover a booking and buy it in from another haulier.

Subcontracting vs your own fleet work

Your own fleet work means your own customers and your own haulage contracts. Subcontracting is a faster way to keep the lorry full: you look for loads on a freight exchange such as TIMOCOM, or take bookings directly from a larger haulier. The risk is lower, but so is the margin, and you often have to wait for payment from the client rather than the end customer. Your own customer often pays within 14 days, whereas a subcontracting client may stretch payment to 30–45 days unless the contract says otherwise. For a small operator this difference is critical for cash flow, which is why negotiating the payment term is part of the bread and butter of subcontracting.

Who is the subcontractor and who is the client

The subcontractor carries out the haulage and is responsible for its own driving, its driver and their working time. The client looks after the customer relationship and pays the agreed price. The interface lies where the invoice and the consignment note change hands. RAHTIS handles both directions in one place, and you can apply to join straight away.

The client is also responsible for customer communication and for any compensation claims if the subcontractor is late. The subcontractor, for its part, is responsible for ensuring the vehicle is legal, insurance is in order and the driver is qualified. Keeping these two roles apart matters, because both liabilities and costs are split differently from those with your own customers.

How does the subcontracting process work, step by step?

Step 1: post your availability and search

Start by posting your own vehicle availability on a freight exchange such as Trans.eu or TIMOCOM. Include the route, vehicle type, capacity and when the vehicle is free. At the same time, browse return loads for your route to keep empty running to a minimum. Keep your search up to date: a load found in the morning is usually cheaper and better than what's left in the afternoon.

Update your search at least twice a day, because many clients publish new loads overnight or early in the morning. If you run a regular route, make the search recurring so that you see loads on that route automatically without browsing by hand.

Step 2: quote and confirmation

When you find a load, quote quickly but check first: mileage, waiting times, loading times and unloading. Price on real costs, not wishful thinking. Always get the confirmation in writing, even if you agreed it by phone first. A written confirmation can be a simple email stating the route, price, loading and delivery addresses and the schedule.

Make sure the rate confirmation also arrives before the job, not after delivery. If the client doesn't send it in time, insist that the job doesn't start without it: without a rate confirmation you have no written proof of the agreed price, and in a dispute it's your word against theirs, which leaves you in a weak position.

Step 3: transport and POD

Stay in touch with the client during the job and collect the POD (the signed consignment note) as soon as the goods are unloaded. The POD is the most important document, because without it the invoice often goes unpaid. If the delivery point won't give you a signed paper copy, ask for a digital acknowledgement or at the very least a photo of the unloaded goods and the recipient's name. Send the POD to the client the same day, not a week later: fast delivery also speeds up invoicing and payment. If the client uses its own TMS, check whether you can upload the POD directly to their portal so the paperwork doesn't get lost in an email thread.

Step 4: invoicing and payment tracking

Once you have the POD, send the invoice with the consignment note, the quote confirmation and any extra charges attached. Track the payment term actively: note who pays and when. RAHTIS combines the quote, POD and invoice into one record and shows unpaid invoices in a single list, so you don't need to keep a paper notebook of who owes what. The RAHTIS API documentation explains how the system connects to other tools if you want to export the data to your own systems.

Invoice within 24 hours of receiving the POD at the latest, because the longer you leave it, the more likely payment is to be late. Add late-payment interest and collection costs to the invoice so that they apply without a separate reminder.

What are the most common subcontracting challenges?

For a small haulage firm, subcontracting problems come down to four things: late deliveries, dispatch run on paper, separate logins to freight exchanges, and nobody knowing who is paying when.

Late delivery: what it costs

When a subcontractor's lorry arrives late, the cost doesn't stop at the driver's day. Your own customer raises a claim, you have to enforce the penalty clause against the subcontractor, and trust is lost. Without a shared schedule and event tracking, you only find out about the delay when the customer rings. In RAHTIS the delivery status is visible in a single view, and through the API the status can also be shared with the customer (API documentation).

The true cost of a late delivery is higher than a single claim: next time the job goes to another subcontractor, and a bad reputation spreads quickly in a trade where clients all know each other. That is why tracking late deliveries systematically matters: if one subcontractor is repeatedly late, the next request for quotation goes to someone else first.

Separate freight exchanges and a paper notebook

The typical set-up: TIMOCOM, Trans.eu and Teleroute, each with its own login, an outdated TMS and a notebook for recording who owes what. Every search and request for quotation takes three logins, the trail of a load is lost as soon as the paperwork changes hands, and when every subcontracted load lives in its own email thread, nobody remembers a week later which client the load belonged to and at what price it was agreed. The problem with a paper notebook isn't just lost information; it's also that nobody can say at the end of the month how much subcontracting was done, what the average payment time was or what share was empty running. Comparing payment times by client also becomes guesswork, even though that is exactly the information that decides who gets the next load and from whom you should demand payment up front. Without these figures you can't negotiate better rates or spot loss-making routes, which is why consolidating into one tool removes half of this work and brings the monthly figures back into view without separate bookkeeping: when the same tool shows searches, PODs and unpaid invoices in one view, the monthly review takes minutes rather than hours, and decisions are based on figures rather than memory.

How do you choose the right subcontractor?

There are three criteria: reliability, working time discipline and vehicles. Always ask for evidence of previous jobs and check that the subcontractor's operator licence details and insurance are in order before the first load. Also check that the subcontractor has valid CMR insurance covering carrier's liability on international journeys.

Ask for a copy of the insurance certificate and check the sum covered: if the goods are worth €50,000 and the insurance only covers 8.33 SDR per kilogram (Special Drawing Rights, roughly €10,000), the subcontractor's cover falls short of the carrier's liability. As the client, that means you bear the difference. That's why checking insurance is just as important as checking the driver's licences.

The "50 working hours" rule and driver workload

A driver's hours are not just the subcontractor's problem: as the client, you are responsible for ensuring the load doesn't push the driver into excess hours or into their rest periods. The EU driving and rest time rules can be found directly in the EU legislation database, and it's worth requiring the subcontractor to provide a timesheet extract with every job. If they can't show the hours, the load isn't theirs.

Timesheets aren't just bureaucracy: if the subcontractor's driver exceeds their hours and gets caught, the whole job can be delayed by days, and you answer to the end customer. Timesheets are therefore risk management as well as compliance. Ask for them in digital form, not on paper, so you can check them quickly before handing over the load.

Which contract terms deserve attention in subcontracting?

Three terms matter most: the payment term, the division of liability and penalties for late delivery.

On payment, hold out for 14 days or less, and agree late-payment interest and reimbursement of collection costs. Also state who pays the freight exchange and broker fees, so that a scrap of paper headed "who owes what" isn't your only record. Check, too, whether the price includes VAT or whether it's added separately, and whether the rate is per kilometre or a fixed price per job. Writing these details down saves explanations later, when the invoice arrives and the total doesn't match what was expected.

On liability, specify whether the subcontractor's liability insurance covers the full carrier's liability, and agree how the excess is split. Include a requirement for drivers to produce their Driver CPC qualification card and working time records. Also define who is responsible for loading and unloading: if the subcontractor's driver has to unload alone and it takes three hours, who pays for the waiting time? Agree waiting times in advance: for example, two hours included in the price, then €30–50 per hour after that.

Penalties for late delivery should be tied to a specific time, not to a "reasonable delay", with the exceptions defined: congestion, weather and unloading delays at the customer's end. For example: "For delays of more than 2 hours beyond the agreed time, excluding force majeure, the subcontractor shall pay 5% of the haulage price per hour or part thereof." This is precise, measurable and easy to apply in a dispute. The alternative, "reasonable compensation", leads to endless negotiation in which each party interprets what is reasonable from its own point of view.

Tie the terms to practice: require the POD and rate confirmation for every job and attach them to the invoice, whatever tool you use. Without attachments an invoice is just an invoice; with them it is proof of performance, and proof speeds up payment.

How do you track and measure subcontracting?

Tracking needs only four metrics, and three of them come straight from RAHTIS records without any extra work.

Punctuality: how many collections and deliveries were on time. When every job is logged in the system, the percentage calculates itself at monthly level. The target is above 95%: if it's lower, either the subcontractors or your own drivers need a word. Punctuality is also a bargaining chip: when you can show a subcontractor that their punctuality is 80% while others manage 96%, the next request for quotation goes to someone else without lengthy explanations.

Payment time: how many days pass from the invoice and POD acknowledgement until the money arrives. RAHTIS tracks the days on unpaid invoices, so you can see at a glance who is late paying. An average of more than 30 days means either your contracts are too loose or your clients are struggling to pay. Under 14 days is a realistic target if you've chosen your clients carefully and your contracts include late-payment interest.

Empty running: how many kilometres you drive without a load. When searching freight exchanges (Trans.eu, TIMOCOM, Teleroute), always check for a return load before quoting; empty running is measured as a share of total distance. The target is under 15%: over 20% means route planning is lacking or subcontracted jobs are too often taken one way without a return load. Every percentage point of empty running comes straight off your profit margin, so this is a figure worth knowing precisely.

Comparison: compare subcontractors' prices and punctuality against each other on the same route. If one is consistently more expensive than the rest, the next request for quotation goes to someone else first. In the same way, compare your own prices with the market: if subcontractors are offering the same route 20% cheaper, either your costs are too high or your price is too low to compete.

These four figures tell you which subcontractors are worth keeping and which of your own customers pay slowest. A monthly review is enough: there's no need to track them daily, but knowing these figures at the end of the month makes the difference between being able to negotiate better terms or not.

FAQ

How much does subcontracted haulage pay on a freight exchange in Finland?

On a freight exchange, the price is set by competitive bidding, so there is no fixed list price. The margin left to the client is typically thinner than on your own customer contracts. The price depends on mileage, waiting and loading times, and unloading. Always price on real costs and agree in writing in advance who pays the freight exchange and broker fees.
