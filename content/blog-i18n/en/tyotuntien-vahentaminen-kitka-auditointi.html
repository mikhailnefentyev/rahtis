You cut working hours by removing friction: unnecessary admin, overlapping tools and hand-compiled reports that eat up your evenings without a single lorry running any more. This guide gives you a step-by-step, measurable friction audit for listing, measuring and prioritising the sources of friction. The result: fewer hours in the office, the same or greater volume on the road.

What does cutting working hours mean in practice in a small firm?

Cutting working hours here does not mean redundancies or driving fewer kilometres. It means cutting your own admin: scraps of paper, separate invoicing and dispatch programs, and evenings spent working out who has paid and who has not.

Why cutting working hours does not mean fewer jobs

A haulage firm's output is the jobs it runs, and those should be left alone. What should be cut is everything else: searching for loads on different exchanges, collecting signed delivery notes, putting invoices together and remembering which invoices are unpaid. Many owner-operators work long weeks, and much of that time goes on things other than the actual business.

How does reducing friction cut working hours?

Friction or real work: a definition

Friction is recurring work that takes time but generates neither freight revenue nor customer relationships. Raising invoices by hand, matching signed delivery notes and transport orders to attach to the invoice, separate logins for every freight exchange and a notebook in which you track who owes whom are all friction. Real work is dispatch decisions, negotiating with customers and keeping the lorries on schedule. The rule is simple: if a task repeats on every load and requires no judgement, it can be handed over to a tool. In RAHTIS, the driver signs off the delivery in the app, an invoice to your own customer is created from the job, and a reminder is sent for an unpaid invoice.

Why friction grows as the firm grows

A one-lorry firm can get by on memory and email, because there are few jobs. With five or twelve lorries, every job multiplies the same admin: more invoices, more exchange searches and more outstanding receivables. Admin grows with the number of jobs, but there are still only so many hours in a day. That is why removing friction is the best way to cut working hours without reducing the volume of haulage.

How do I identify the biggest sources of friction?

A friction audit starts with one week and one notebook. Record every recurring admin task: calls to drivers, searching exchanges for loads, invoicing, collecting signed delivery notes, checking hours and rest periods. No estimates, just the actual minutes.

Step 1: Log a week's admin tasks

Keep your phone or a sheet of paper within reach all week. Note each task as soon as you do it, not from memory in the evening. By the end of the week you will have a list that surprises you: many people find tasks they had never thought of as "work" at all.

Step 2: Note frequency and duration

Two figures for each line: how many times a week, and how many minutes each time. Multiply them together to get the task's weekly hours. This is the figure you will compare later when choosing tools.

Step 3: Separate friction from valuable work

Customer work, negotiating rates and safety matters are valuable work. Copying data from one system to another, entering the same load in three places and working out "who has paid" are friction. Do not count drivers' rest periods as friction: the EU driving and rest time regulation (Regulation (EC) No 561/2006) requires them, and monitoring them is statutory work, not unnecessary admin. Once the list is complete, the job of a tool is to remove entirely the lines that are nothing but copying.

In what order should you cut working hours?

Scoring: frequency, duration, risk of error

Score each task found in the audit on three scales: how often it recurs (daily = 3, weekly = 2, monthly = 1), how long it takes each time (over 30 min = 3, 10–30 min = 2, under 10 min = 1) and how easily mistakes happen (an amount to be invoiced or a sign-off = 3, data transfer = 2, a to-do list = 1). Multiply the figures together. The highest score tells you where to start.

An example: the "who has paid" notebook recurs daily (3), often takes over half an hour (3) and a mistake costs money directly (3), so it scores 27. A monthly transport report that takes a quarter of an hour and where a mistake is harmless scores 1 × 2 × 1 = 2. The order is clear.

The order: duplicated work first, then manual transfer, reporting last

Fix duplicated work first, because it removes two tasks at once: when the job details, your own vehicle and the invoice are in one place, you no longer copy the same transport order into two systems. This is the point at which many small haulage firms find that consolidating tools pays for itself fastest: separate exchange logins, an old program and a notebook are replaced by a workflow in which the job is entered once, the driver signs off the delivery, the invoice goes out and payment is tracked. In RAHTIS, this chain runs from job to invoice; jobs come from your own customers and the RAHTIS load board, but it does not bring in loads from other freight exchanges. It is worth reading the terms of use before committing to any system. Second, make manual data transfer, such as recording drivers' hours or refuelling, electronic. Reporting comes last, because it is done least often and is the easiest to replace with a ready-made report or export.

Which tools remove the most friction for a small haulage firm?

Separate freight exchanges or an integrated system

In most small firms, friction comes from three places: separate exchange logins, a separate transport management program or Excel, and a scrap of paper recording who has paid. TIMOCOM, Teleroute and Trans.eu take care of finding loads, but invoicing and signed delivery notes remain separate jobs, and you glue the systems together in the evenings.

An integrated system means that the same tool shows the jobs, records the runs, stores the driver's sign-off and generates the invoice. When the transport order, sign-off and invoice are in the same chain, the "who still owes us" folder disappears, and you no longer have to copy data from one program to another in the evenings.

How does cutting working hours affect volume and quality?

Removing friction does not reduce the number of jobs. You have just as many lorries, but the time spent on admin is freed up for other things: sales, customer service or rest. When invoices and signed delivery notes are compiled in one place, errors decrease and invoices reach the customer sooner, which speeds up payment.

What to use the freed-up hours for

Decide in advance where the freed-up hours will go. Three realistic options for a small haulage firm:

  1. Sales and customers. Call one more customer a week, or reprice your regular customers' fixed routes.
  2. Backloads. Actively look for backloads, so empty running falls and every kilometre earns more.
  3. Rest. An overloaded owner makes mistakes in dispatch and invoicing. Staying fresh is a quality factor in itself.

Quality improves from the customer's point of view too: when the status of jobs and payments is clear without paper notes, the phone rings less often and answers come faster. Fewer hours are spent, but delivery reliability and invoicing accuracy improve.

How do you track the hours saved without adding more admin?

One measure is enough

Once a month, record a single figure: admin hours per week. Compare it with the level measured before the changes. If the figure falls and the volume of haulage has not dropped, the change worked. If you have no baseline, estimate it in the first month, even roughly.

The same principle works when comparing tools. When you compare freight exchanges such as TIMOCOM, Trans.eu or Teleroute, the measure is the same: how many minutes it takes to find and record one load, and how many empty kilometres were left over each week.

Do not build reporting around the tracking. If collecting the measure takes more than five minutes a month, it is a new source of friction, not a solution.

Which mistakes slow down cutting working hours?

Too many changes at once

The most common mistake is trying to fix everything at once. If you change your invoicing program, freight exchange and dispatch routines in the same month, you will not know what worked and what did not. Pick one source of friction, such as the "who has paid" notebook, and sort it out before moving on to the next.

The second pitfall is overlapping tools. Separate exchange logins, an old program and a scrap of paper do the same work three times over. Consolidating tools only cuts hours if you drop the old way of working at the same time.

Mistaking breaks and rest for friction

The third mistake is cutting in the wrong place. Meal breaks, enough sleep and drivers' rest periods are not friction but a precondition for safe, good-quality work. Drivers' breaks and rest periods are statutory obligations and are not up for negotiation.

Forgetting to measure is the fourth stumbling block. If you do not record the hours for weeks 1–4, you will not see whether the time spent on invoicing went down or simply moved somewhere else.

FAQ

How many hours can a small haulage firm realistically save?

That depends on the starting point, which is why the one-week log is essential. In the example table, three tasks took almost six hours a week just for exchange searches, invoicing and calls. Add the other recurring routines, and admin can come to around ten hours a week, part of which is copying that can be eliminated.

Should a small firm adopt a transport management system or keep doing things by hand?

The deciding question is whether the tool removes lines from your audit list. Maintaining an old program and separate exchanges can itself be friction if the data still have to be combined by hand in the evenings. A system pays for itself fastest when the job is entered once and invoicing, signed delivery notes and payment tracking follow from the same chain.

How do I start the audit if I don't even have time for that?

Start smaller than you think: keep your phone in your pocket and note each task as soon as you do it. A note takes seconds, not a separate work session. If even that seems impossible, measure one day and multiply the figures by five. A rough baseline is enough for comparison.

Will revenue fall if working hours are cut?

Not in this model, because the kilometres driven are not reduced. The time cut is admin that generated no freight revenue. The freed-up hours can go on sales, finding backloads or rest, so the volume of haulage stays the same or grows, invoicing errors decrease and payments come in faster.
