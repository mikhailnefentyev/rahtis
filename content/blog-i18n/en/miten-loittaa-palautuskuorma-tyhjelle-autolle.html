What is a return load and why is an empty lorry a cost risk?

A return load, or backhaul, is freight you take on for the journey home when your own customer's job or a freight exchange job ends in the wrong place. Empty running (a trailer with no load) means the same journey with no income. Freight exchanges such as Trans.eu and Teleroute list these return loads all the time, and RAHTIS matches the empty legs of your own fleet directly with jobs on offer (https://www.rahtis.eu/fi).

Empty running is not just fuel: it is lost margin

An empty lorry has the same cost structure as a loaded one: fuel, the driver's wages, insurance, tyres and depreciation all run, but no money comes in. In European road haulage roughly a fifth of vehicle-kilometres are driven empty, which is capacity wasted outright (Eurostat, Road freight transport statistics).

When you run 300 kilometres empty, you do not just lose fuel. You lose the amount the return load would have brought in, less fuel and tolls. That is why looking for return loads should be a routine, not hit-and-miss hunting.

Which channels should you search for return loads first, and in what order?

It pays to dig for return loads in a set order: your own customers first, then regular partners, then freight exchanges and terminals last. The order is based on where you get the best price and the least risk relative to the time spent searching.

1. Your own customers and regular partners

Call or message the customers and subcontracting partners whose routes cross the route of your empty lorry. You already know how they pay and what equipment they need, so the negotiation takes a minute. Ask in particular about regular loads on set weekdays: one fixed return load a week is worth more than a one-off load.

2. Freight exchanges and load boards

If your own network turns up nothing, open the freight exchanges. Those used in Finland and Northern Europe include Teleroute, TIMOCOM, Trans.eu and Maxmove; an option that combines transport planning and load search is RAHTIS (https://www.rahtis.eu/sv). The biggest difference between the channels is how quickly you see new loads along your route.

3. Terminals, ports and loose trailer traffic

Last of all, check the terminals and ports along the route: loose trailer and container loads often only appear on the day. These carry the highest risk, because waiting times and payment terms vary, so take them only when the other channels have come up empty.

How do route and timing affect which loads are worth offering to carry?

The direction of the route, when loads are posted and how flexible the load is decide which return loads are realistically available. The same lorry gets different results on a different day and on a different corridor.

Route direction and the rhythm of port traffic

Empty running is most likely in the direction where the least freight moves. Loose trailers leaving ports and industrial hubs head inland on weekday mornings, so an empty lorry at a port in the afternoon is competing for very few loads. The opposite direction, from rural areas to the hubs, often offers more jobs but lower rates. Equipment narrows the choice: a reefer load will not be offered to a flatbed, and a required loading time rules out long repositioning runs.

Timing: when loads are posted and when they disappear

Freight exchanges such as Teleroute and TIMOCOM post loads around the clock, but in practice new offers tend to appear in the mornings and afternoons, and urgent ones are gone within hours. If your lorry will be empty at 12 noon tomorrow, offer to take loads whose collection time is flexible, and keep your phone free for the morning posting window. Jobs published in RAHTIS are visible step by step, so you can follow the shipper's schedule changes without calling; the terms of use set out which party notifies cancellations and when (https://www.rahtis.eu/fi/kayttoehdot). Speed of response often matters more than price.

How do you decide whether to take a return load or run home empty?

An empty journey home always costs money: fuel, time and wear accumulate even though there is no income. A return load is almost always worth taking if it covers the variable costs and leaves even a small margin. The sum is simple: the freight rate offered minus fuel, tolls and extra working hours. If what is left is more than the cost of running empty, the load wins.

Decision tree: four questions before you quote

Before quoting, go through four points in this order. The first is the route: does the load take you towards your next job or your home base, or do you have to go 200 km out of your way, making the actual journey longer? The second is timing: can loading and unloading be done before the job you have already agreed, and is there enough buffer for delays? The third is equipment: do the trailer type, lifting gear or heating meet the load's requirements, or would you need extra kit you do not otherwise carry? The fourth is payment terms: ask about the payment period straight away, and if it is 60 days or more, price the delay in or do not quote. If any of the four fails, running home empty is often cheaper than taking a bad load.

Checklist: equipment, weight, time, payment terms

Before quoting, check: the total weight and floor area of the load against your own equipment, the loading and unloading times (unpaid hours can easily eat the margin), tolls and ferry fares on the route, and the consignment note and CMR details in advance. Also bear in mind the risk that there will be no next load ready at the delivery point.

All in all, the decision is the same every time: work out the margin, compare it with the cost of running empty and decide within five minutes. RAHTIS shows the route, timing and payment terms of a job in the same view, so comparing is quicker than leafing through a notebook and separate exchange logins (https://www.rahtis.eu/et). The system does not make the decision for you, but it removes the searching, which is often the reason bad loads are taken in a rush.

What should you put in a load offer to get a reply?

An offer that gets a reply contains five points: equipment (e.g. crane or refrigerated trailer, payload), availability window in hours, route from collection to delivery and return, price and driver details. Write the price clearly: is it fixed or negotiable, and does it include waiting time?

Add one line that makes the biggest difference: "Available [date, time] on [town]–[town], equipment [specify], price [€]." A vague "we have space" is ignored, because the shipper is comparing dozens of offers within minutes.

Driver details and liability issues are best agreed in writing at the offer stage, since the entries in the consignment note bind the carrier under the liability rules of the CMR Convention (CMR material from the Finnish transport and logistics sector). In RAHTIS the offer and the job details travel in the same system, so no paper is needed; the features are listed in more detail in the product description.

How do you avoid the payment and liability risks of return loads?

A return load from a freight exchange often comes from an unknown shipper, so check their ability to pay before you commit. Ask directly about the payment period and, if necessary, ask for payment in advance on the first job. In Finland, late payment interest and the right to recovery costs are laid down in the Interest Act (korkolaki) (Finlex, Interest Act), so collecting overdue freight charges is not merely a matter for negotiation. Keep a record of each customer's payment times so you notice who repeatedly pays late.

Payment terms and credit information

The Finnish Trade Register shows that a company exists, but payment default information should be checked separately in a payment default register before a larger job. A security deposit or a trading account rating on the exchange (e.g. Trans.eu or TIMOCOM) gives an indication of how the shipper pays, but it does not replace your own judgement. Running home empty is sometimes cheaper than freight paid 60 days late.

CMR and liability for the load

Fill in the CMR consignment note carefully: the quantity as counted at dispatch and any remarks on the apparent condition of the goods protect you in the event of damage. Under the CMR Convention, the exemptions available to the carrier limit your liability, but exceptions (e.g. an incorrect description by the shipper) should be recorded on the document itself. A paper notebook is not enough as evidence if a dispute arises only weeks later.

How do you turn finding return loads into a regular routine rather than hit-and-miss hunting?

Hit-and-miss hunting means you open the exchange only when the lorry is already empty and the phone is ringing. A routine means that fleet status, alerts and the order of work are ready before you need them. Three steps are enough.

Recording fleet status in advance

Every morning, write down which lorry is where, where it is heading back to, when it will be free and what equipment it has (trailer, crane, temperature requirement). This takes five minutes and is the foundation of the whole routine, because without it you do not know what return load you are actually looking for. A paper notebook will do, but it is worth having the same information on your phone as well.

Alerts and filters on the exchange

Do not browse the freight exchange by hand. Set filters for your usual routes: origin, destination direction, date and equipment requirement, and switch on alerts for new postings. When an offer comes in, reply quickly with a complete offer whose structure is already ready from previous jobs.

One tool instead of several exchanges

Several separate exchange and TMS logins are exactly the mess that eats up a one-person planner's day. In RAHTIS you publish a job once, follow it step by step and handle consignment notes and invoicing in the same place. To be honest, RAHTIS does not yet bring all external freight exchanges together in one view the way larger systems do, so some of the exchange checking remains your responsibility. The routine stays the same though: record your fleet, let the alerts do the work for you and reply quickly.

FAQ

The return load does not cover the whole journey – is it still worth taking?

Often, yes. Work out the margin for the whole journey: the freight rate offered minus fuel, tolls and extra working hours, and compare the result with the cost of the empty kilometres. If a part-journey load covers the variable costs and leaves even a small margin, it beats running empty. The exception is a detour that lengthens the overall journey too much.

How long before an empty run should you start looking for a return load?

As soon as you know when the lorry will be free and what the return route is, which is often a day or two before the empty run. Loads appear on the exchanges in the mornings and afternoons, and urgent ones are gone within hours. Set up filters and alerts in advance so that you see new loads straight away without constant browsing.

Can a return load be priced lower than normal freight, and how do I justify the price?

Yes, it can, because the lorry is heading back anyway. Justify the offer by the fact that the load fits a route and time already planned: collection and delivery fit the agreed times, the equipment is the right type and there is no extra mileage. State the price precisely and say whether waiting time is included, so that the shipper can compare easily.

What do I do if the shipper of a return load does not pay?

Ask about the payment period at the offer stage and keep a record of customers' payment times. In Finland, late payment interest on overdue freight charges and the right to recovery costs are based on the Interest Act, so collection is not merely a matter for negotiation. For larger jobs, check the payment default register in advance; if the payment period is 60 days or more, price the delay in or do not quote.

Do I need a separate contract or a CMR for a return load taken from an exchange?

A CMR consignment note is always needed, and it should be filled in carefully: the quantity as counted at dispatch and any remarks on the apparent condition of the goods protect you if there is damage. Driver details and liability issues are best agreed in writing at the offer stage, since the liability rules of the CMR Convention are binding. Each exchange's terms of use supplement the contract but do not replace the entries in the consignment note.
