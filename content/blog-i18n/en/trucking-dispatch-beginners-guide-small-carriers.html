Trucking dispatch is the daily work of finding loads on a freight exchange, confirming rates, assigning them to your drivers, checking in on progress, and collecting the CMR notes and PODs so you get paid. This guide walks through one full dispatch day step by step, then helps you decide whether to hire a dispatcher, use software, or keep doing it yourself.

What does a trucking dispatcher actually do day to day?

A dispatcher fills empty trucks, keeps drivers moving, and turns completed jobs into paid invoices. In a 1-12 truck company, that person is often you, doing it between driving, quoting, and chasing paperwork.

The morning: filling empty trucks from the load board

The day starts with the freight exchange or load board: matching trucks that would otherwise run empty (tyhjäajo) against loads that pay well and end somewhere useful for a return load. That means several logins, comparing rates, and booking keikka before drivers finish their morning coffee.

The middle of the day: driver communication and check calls

Once trucks roll, dispatch is answering calls: delays at the terminal, a changed loading time, a detour. Each change means updating the schedule, telling the shipper, and sometimes rebooking a slot. It is reactive work, and it eats the hours you planned for selling.

The end of the day: paperwork, PODs, and who owes what

Evenings go to CMR notes, PODs, and the notebook tracking who owes what. One 4-truck owner described exactly this mess: separate load board logins, an old TMS, and a paper notebook for receivables (Reddit thread). Tools like RAHTIS exist to collapse that loop into one place.

A real dispatch workflow, step by step

Step 1: Search the freight exchange for a return load

Your morning starts with the freight exchange, not your email. You filter by the lane your truck will be empty on, check the weight and trailer type the cargo needs, and shortlist two or three offers worth a call. If you run your own clients, you first check which keikka is already booked, then search the rahtipörssi only for the gap that would otherwise be tyhjäajo. The goal is simple: never let a truck leave a region without a return load confirmed.

Step 2: Quote, agree the price, and get the rate confirmation

You quote based on the lane, the loading windows, and what the customer accepted last time. Once the price is agreed, get it in writing, whether that is a rate confirmation from a broker or an accepted offer on the exchange. A verbal "yes, we'll load you at 14:00" is not a rate confirmation. This document is what your invoice rests on later.

Step 3: Send the job to the driver and set check-in times

Pass the driver the essentials: pickup address, time window, cargo details, contact number, and CMR requirements. Set fixed check-in times, typically at loading and at delivery, so you are not chasing updates by phone all day. Write the check-ins into your dispatch sheet so a missed call is visible within the hour.

Step 4: Handle exceptions: delays, trailer swaps, terminal queues

The plan breaks at the terminal queue, the late shipper, or the trailer that suddenly needs swapping. Your job is to rebook the delivery window, tell the customer before they ask, and log what changed. A dispatcher who documents exceptions calmly is the reason customers come back.

Step 5: Close the job: CMR, POD, invoice, and tracking payment

The job is not done at delivery. You collect the signed CMR and POD, match them to the rate confirmation, invoice, and then track days-to-payment until the money lands. What trucking dispatch terminology do beginners need to know?

Load board, freight exchange, rahtipörssi: same thing, different words

A load board is a marketplace where shippers and forwarders post loads and carriers post available trucks. In Northern Europe the same service is called a freight exchange or rahtipörssi, and the big ones are Teleroute, TIMOCOM and Trans.eu, each requiring its own login (https://www.teleroute.com). Irtoperä means a semitrailer hauled without its own tractor: the shipper or port hands you a loaded trailer and you supply only the tractor and driver. A keikka is a single job.

Empty running, irtoperä, tyhjäajo, backhaul

Tyhjäajo is a truck driving empty after a drop-off. Dispatchers search the exchange for a return load or backhaul to cover those kilometres; an irtoperä trailer heading your way can be that backhaul, since the tractor going home empty only has to hook it up. RAHTIS (rahtis.eu) puts its own load board, dispatch board and CMR paperwork in one place; it does not pull in loads from other exchanges, so those still need their own logins.

Should a small carrier hire a dispatcher, use dispatch software, or do it themselves?

What hiring a dispatcher costs and when it makes sense

A hired dispatcher is usually paid a percentage of each load's revenue, so on thin Finnish domestic rates that cut can eat most of your margin on irtoperä work. It makes sense when you are sitting in the cab all day and genuinely cannot search the rahtipörssi or answer driver calls. If you are already office-based part of the day, paying someone to do what you could do yourself is hard to justify.

What dispatch software must do for a 1-12 truck fleet

At your size, the software replaces the notebook, not a person. It must handle load searching and booking, driver assignment, CMR paperwork, and tracking who owes what, without per-user pricing that punishes a one-man dispatch office. Tools like Transportial or Trans.eu cover parts of this; a full workflow in one place is what RAHTIS is built for, from published job to paid invoice. Honest limitation: RAHTIS will not negotiate your rates or chase a broker by phone, so you still do the talking.

The honest case for doing it yourself with the right tools

Dispatching your own trucks keeps the margin and builds your customer base directly. The workload is real, roughly an hour or two daily on planning and paperwork, but software that consolidates load boards, dispatch, and invoicing makes that manageable for one person. Start by doing it yourself; hire help only when trucks are sitting idle because you cannot answer the phone.

How does dispatching differ for hotshot trucking versus full-size semi fleets?

Hotshot is the American term for a pickup and gooseneck trailer running smaller, urgent loads. In Finland and Northern Europe the closest equivalent is the van or light truck chasing small part loads and short keikka off the rahtipörssi.

Smaller loads, faster turns

A hotshot dispatcher books more, shorter jobs per day, so the phone work and paperwork multiply even though revenue per load is smaller. Empty running hurts more too: with a light trailer, a return load matters on nearly every trip, not just occasionally. Each international job still needs a CMR note: the UNECE CMR Convention applies to international road carriage for reward regardless of vehicle size, while domestic jobs in Finland run on an ordinary rahtikirja under the Finnish Road Transport Contracts Act (tiekuljetussopimuslaki).

What carries over to any fleet size

The core loop is identical: search the load board, agree the rate, dispatch, track, invoice. RAHTIS handles that loop the same way whether you run one van or twelve semis; on fast-turn freight, expect more calls whatever tool you use.

What does free trucking dispatch software include, and when is it not enough?

What free tiers and spreadsheets actually cover

A spreadsheet or notebook can hold load details, driver names, and who owes what. That is exactly what the 4-truck owner on Reddit described: separate load board logins, an old TMS, and a paper notebook for receivables (Reddit thread). Free tiers of dispatch tools usually add job lists and basic status tracking. That covers the first half of a dispatch day, not the paperwork half.

Where free tools break: per-user pricing, load boards, invoicing

Free tools stop where the money moves. Rate confirmations, CMR notes, POD collection, and invoicing usually sit behind paid plans, and per-user pricing hurts when you are the dispatcher, accountant, and safety guy in one. Load boards also stay separate: you still log into Teleroute, TIMOCOM, or Trans.eu to find irtoperä and backhaul loads, then retype the job into your dispatch sheet.

How do you train to become a trucking dispatcher without prior experience?

Trucking dispatch classes and courses: what they teach

Paid dispatch courses typically cover load board searching, rate confirmation basics, broker communication, and paperwork like the CMR note. They are useful if you have never touched a freight exchange or a rahtipörssi listing. What they cannot teach is your own lanes, your own drivers, and your own customers, which is where most of the real learning happens anyway.

Learning on your own fleet: a 30-day plan

Week 1: document every job you run, from search to POD, on paper. Week 2: move that paper into one place, replacing the notebook with a spreadsheet or a TMS so who-owes-what stops living in your head. Week 3: standardise check-in times with drivers and file every rate con and rahtikirja the same way. Week 4: review the month, find the slowest step, and fix only that one.

What is the difference between a freight exchange and a TMS?

A freight exchange, or rahtipörssi, is a marketplace where shippers post loads and carriers post available trucks; Teleroute, TIMOCOM, and Trans.eu are examples. A TMS is your internal system for running jobs: driver assignment, CMR paperwork, and tracking who owes what. Most small carriers juggle both separately; some tools now bundle them in one place.
