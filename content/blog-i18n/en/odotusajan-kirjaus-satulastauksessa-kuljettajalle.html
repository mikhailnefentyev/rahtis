The driver records waiting time during a trailer swap as working time by noting the start and end time, the location of the terminal or port, the nature of the task (trailer swap, container swap or waiting for the load) and a reference such as the consignment note number. This guide shows step by step how to make the entry, what a sample entry that stands up to payroll and working time inspection looks like, and on what grounds a waiting time entry is rejected. Recording waiting time during trailer swaps is closely regulated, and done properly it protects both the driver's pay and the employer's invoicing.

When is waiting time during a trailer swap working time?

Often, but not always. When a driver is swapping a trailer or container at a terminal or port and has to wait for loading, unloading or for the replacement unit to become available, the wait is working time if they have to stay on site at the employer's disposal, even though the vehicle is not moving and the driver is not doing physical work. Under section 3 of the Finnish Working Hours Act (872/2019), working time is time spent working and time during which the employee is obliged to be present at the workplace at the employer's disposal (Finnish Occupational Safety and Health Administration). The collective agreement for the truck sector makes this more specific: if the driver has to move the truck repeatedly on the orders of the loader or unloader, the time is working time, but if they are free to leave the truck, the wait is paid as waiting-time pay and does not count as working time (section 11, point 12).

The practical significance of this rule is considerable. If the driver has stayed at the terminal for their daily rest but is woken in the middle of it to move the truck when the trailer becomes available, that rest is no longer uninterrupted. The rest period is then broken and cannot count as daily rest, and the driver is back on working time. This means that recording waiting time during trailer swaps is not a mere formality: it determines whether the rest is considered complete or not. Without an entry, the driver and the employer risk misreading the situation and breaching the working time rules.

When waiting time counts as working time and when it does not

Waiting counts as working time when the trailer swap is part of a task assigned by the employer and the driver has to stay on site. If, on the other hand, the driver waits at the terminal on their own initiative without an assignment, or is outside working hours, the time is not recorded. In borderline cases, what matters is whether the driver had to stay on site or was free to use their time as they wished. The entry can be made by hand or per vehicle in a system such as RAHTIS, where the stages of a job run from the driver all the way to invoicing.

In practice the difference is clear: if the transport planner has told the driver to go to the terminal and wait for a trailer swap, the waiting is working time from the outset. If, instead, the driver decides to stop at the terminal to wait for the next job without any specific instruction, the time cannot be recorded as working time. Likewise, if the driver has said they are reachable but is free to leave the vehicle and use their own time, this is a period of availability, which does not count as working time, even though it may be paid for.

What information must a waiting time entry include?

A rejected waiting time entry is almost always an incomplete one: it lacks the time, the place or the reason. The payroll administrator cannot accept a line saying "at terminal 2 h", because it does not show whether it was waiting for a trailer swap or some other delay.

To make the entry complete, the driver needs to know exactly which fields the payroll administrator and the inspector expect to see. The table below lists the required fields; if any is missing, the entry is rejected automatically. These fields must be filled in for every waiting time entry, whether a paper form, email or a transport management system is used.

Step by step: how the driver records waiting time during a trailer swap

Before arriving at the terminal

Record a time stamp as soon as you set off: take a photo or send a message on your phone showing your departure time and estimated arrival time at the terminal. Make sure the job instructions or consignment note include the customer's contact details so you can report a delay or an early arrival. If the load or trailer is not ready as agreed, report it before you arrive, so the reason for the wait can be verified from the start.

On arrival and when the wait begins

On arrival, record two things: the time and the place. In practice a time-stamped photo of the gate display, the gate sign or the yard, or a location-based entry in the app, is enough. Also record who is receiving you: the terminal name, gate number and any acknowledgement of your arrival. Tell reception you have arrived and ask them to confirm that the load or trailer is not ready, because it is exactly this confirmation that separates an accepted waiting time entry from a rejected one.

Directive 2002/15/EC distinguishes between working time and periods of availability. Waiting for loading or unloading also counts as working time when the driver has to stay at their workstation and the foreseeable duration of the wait is not known in advance. A period of availability, which does not count as working time, is a wait during which the driver is not required to remain at their workstation and whose foreseeable duration is known in advance (Article 3 of the directive on EUR-Lex). So record the start of the wait as soon as reception tells you the load is not there, and note whether you were told in advance how long the wait would be: do not wait to see whether it sorts itself out within the hour. Try recording waiting time in RAHTIS yourself: https://www.rahtis.eu/sv.

When the wait ends and recording what follows

Once the trailer has been swapped or the load secured, note the end time of the wait and continue the journey on the same entry. Finally, record the trailer or container number, the consignment note number and any acknowledgement from reception. The entry then forms a continuous chain: arrival, start of waiting, end of waiting and onward journey, all on the same job.

Sample entries: a trailer swap and waiting for a load

Example 1: trailer swap in the terminal yard

An accepted entry might look like this: "10/3, 09:15–10:40, trailer swap, terminal yard, Vantaa. Trailer change: empty trailer collected from the yard, loaded trailer dropped in its place, tractor unit and trailer recoupled. Witness: terminal shift supervisor." Record the time of arrival, not just the time the swap started.

Example 2: waiting at the gate before collecting a container

Another example: "10/3, 12:00 arrived at port gate, collection from the gate began at 13:30, container secured at 14:10. Reason for waiting: container had not yet been grounded." Here the waiting time spent at the gate is 1 h 30 min, and the entry shows the reason, so the person invoicing can claim compensation from the customer.

In both cases it is worth attaching a time-stamped photo of the gate display or the consignment note. When the same entry feeds directly into the job stages in RAHTIS, the driver does not copy information by hand, and the payroll administrator sees the waiting time in the same view as the rest of the job's records (https://www.rahtis.eu/lv).

The most common reasons for rejection in payroll

The most common reason for rejection is a missing vehicle or trailer registration: without a registration number, the payroll administrator cannot match the entry to the driving log record. The second is vague timing, such as just "all morning" with no start and end time. The third is a missing reason: an entry that only says "waiting terminal" does not show whether it was queuing because the load was being prepared or getting ready for the trailer swap.

The fourth reason is a conflict with the digital tachograph. If the tachograph shows rest for the same period for which the driver is claiming waiting time as working time, the payroll administrator will reject the entry unless it is a precisely defined exception. Working time is supervised by the occupational safety and health authority (since the start of 2026, the Finnish Licensing and Supervision Agency), and driving and rest times by the police at roadside checks. In either kind of inspection, discrepancies between the working time records and the tachograph must be explainable, so it is worth adding an explanation when the entry is made.

The fifth reason is a late retrospective entry made weeks afterwards. Avoid all five by recording the waiting time on the same day and adding the trailer swap details: terminal, container number and customer name. In RAHTIS the same entry is attached directly to the job, so invoicing and payroll see the same information without a separate email.

How does the entry stand up to a working time inspection?

The authorities compare waiting time entries with two sources: the driver's working time records and the vehicle's digital tachograph. Questions arise if the tachograph shows a stop during the trailer swap but nothing has been entered in the working time records, or the recorded time differs from the tachograph by hours.

Reconciling the digital tachograph and the manual entry

Record waiting time for the same period as the digital tachograph records. If the trailer swap started at 10:15 and ended at 12:45, the same times go into the working time records, not rounded hours. Explain in the entry why the vehicle was stationary: for example "queuing at terminal, container swap, collecting consignment note".

If the entry and the tachograph do not match, correct both on the same day and record the reason for the correction. Entries rejected in payroll are often ones where the driver's paper note and the system's time stamps differ with no explanation. When the entry, the tachograph and the payroll records tell the same story, the inspection goes through without questions.

The inspector may also ask for evidence that the wait was caused by the customer or the terminal and not by the driver's own delay. That is why photos of gate displays, emails from the sender or terminal receipts are worth their weight in gold. If the waiting time was interrupted by a break or rest, the inspector will want to see that the break was taken in accordance with the rules and that there are no gaps in the working time records. From the inspector's point of view, recording waiting time during trailer swaps is most reliable when every stop is explained and the driving schedule is consistent from departure to destination.

How does the transport planner handle waiting time entries in payroll?

In a small firm, the transport planner and the payroll administrator are often the same person, so it pays to process a waiting time entry as soon as the driver's record comes in. First check that the entry has a start and end time, the name of the terminal or port and the reason for waiting. If any of these is missing, ask for it to be completed the same day, not at the end of the payroll run.

Waiting time in customer invoicing and the freight rate

On the pay side, waiting time is recorded either as working time or as waiting time under the collective agreement, according to the driver's employment contract and the sector's terms. On the invoicing side the basis is different: the transport contract sets out which waiting time is charged as a surcharge and on what basis, for example at an hourly rate after a period of free waiting time. So agree with the customer in advance when free waiting time ends and how waiting is documented; information on the sector's terms of carriage is available, for example, from the haulage operators' association (SKAL).

In practice, then, the same entry is used twice: as working time for payroll and as a surcharge on the freight invoice. When both are based on the same driver entry, the figures also match at inspection.

Where RAHTIS does not help (yet)

RAHTIS does not know your customer-specific waiting time terms, so check the basis for the surcharge in your own transport contract. Nor does the system replace the tachograph or your payroll system; it produces entries that you transfer to your own payroll.

FAQ

Does the customer pay for waiting time if the trailer swap is delayed for reasons on the terminal's side?

Often, yes, but the basis lies in the contract, not in the driver's entry. The transport contract usually sets a period of free waiting time, after which the right to a surcharge arises. Record the reason for the delay, the start and end time and the order reference, so that invoicing can base the surcharge on saved data rather than memory.

Is the digital tachograph enough as evidence of waiting time, or is a separate entry needed?

A separate entry is needed. The digital tachograph shows that the vehicle was stationary, but not the reason or the task. The inspector will have questions if the stop on the tachograph and the working time records do not match. Record the same times, the location, the type of swap and a reference to the consignment note, so that any discrepancy can be explained afterwards.

What do I do if the terminal will not give arrival or release times in writing?

Record the times yourself on the same day and add your own evidence: a time-stamped photo of the consignment note, the name of the consignee or terminal supervisor and the container number. If the wait was caused by a queue, note your arrival time and the time your turn came separately. These supporting details speed up approval even when the terminal confirms nothing in writing.

Is waiting time during a trailer swap recorded as working time for the driver of the vehicle or for the driver doing the swap?

The entry goes into the working time records of the driver who was at the employer's disposal during the wait. If the driver of the replacement vehicle waits on site as instructed, the time is their working time. What decides it is not the vehicle's registration number but who was instructed to be there and whose free time the wait restricted.

How much waiting time is paid to the driver in full, and when is it treated as a break?

No general limit for full payment can be given without an employment contract or the sector's terms: on the pay side, your own contract terms decide. On the tachograph, a long stationary period is easily recorded as rest, so that the working time claim and the tachograph data conflict. Record the start and end of the wait accurately and explain in the entry in advance how breaks fit in.
