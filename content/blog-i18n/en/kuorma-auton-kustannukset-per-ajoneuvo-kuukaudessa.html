A heavy lorry can easily cost €9,000–15,000 a month. In the weightings of Statistics Finland's cost index for road haulage by lorry (base year 2010), drivers' wages including employer contributions account for almost half of costs, fuel for about a fifth, and the rest is capital cost, maintenance, tyres, insurance, taxes and administration. The exact amount depends on the vehicle's age and price, the kilometres driven and whether you drive yourself or employ a driver. This article gives you a step-by-step calculation model and a checklist of cost items, so you can work out your own vehicle's monthly cost instead of relying on averages.

What does a lorry cost per month?

A vehicle's monthly cost has two parts: fixed costs, which you pay even if the lorry is standing in the yard, and variable costs, which depend on the kilometres driven. Fixed costs include capital cost (depreciation and financing), insurance, vehicle tax and fixed-price service contracts. Variable costs include fuel, tyres and mileage-based maintenance. An employed driver is in practice a fixed cost if the wage is paid regardless of how much the lorry runs.

The amount of Finnish vehicle tax depends on the vehicle's power source and weights, and you can check it on the website of Traficom, the Finnish Transport and Communications Agency: Traficom: vehicle taxation.

It pays to keep the same split in your system too. In the RAHTIS profitability view you enter the vehicle's purchase price or finance instalment, insurance, vehicle tax and other fixed costs, and it shows each vehicle's revenue, costs and result month by month: https://www.rahtis.eu/en.

What are the fixed costs you pay even when the lorry is idle?

Fixed costs run by the calendar, not the odometer. Add them up into a single monthly figure per vehicle, because they determine the revenue level below which the vehicle must not fall.

Capital cost and depreciation

The purchase price, less the estimated residual value, divided by the number of months of service life is often the largest single fixed item. If a lorry costs €120,000 and is written off over eight years with no residual value, the capital cost is €1,250 a month. If the vehicle is financed by a loan, hire purchase or leasing, use the monthly instalment including interest in your calculation.

Insurance and vehicle tax

Motor third-party liability insurance, comprehensive (casco) cover and carrier's liability insurance, as well as vehicle tax, are usually billed annually or in a few instalments, so you have to spread them over twelve months yourself. Combine them into a single monthly amount so that nothing catches you out on the due date.

Other fixed costs: parking, inspection, systems

Parking, the periodic roadworthiness inspection (katsastus, the Finnish equivalent of the MOT), the base fees of service contracts, and dispatch and invoicing tools belong in this category. An example breakdown:

ItemExample per month
Capital cost€1,250
Insurance + vehicle tax€450
Parking and inspection€150
Systems (transport management, freight exchanges, tachograph software)€100

You can reduce system costs by cutting overlapping tools. In RAHTIS, fleet, jobs, the load board and invoicing your own customers are in one service; the first vehicle is free and each additional vehicle that has run jobs costs €14.90 a month (excl. VAT). Once your fixed costs are on paper, you know how much each vehicle has to earn before a single litre of fuel has gone into the tank.

Which costs vary with the kilometres driven?

On top of fixed costs, the vehicle incurs mileage-related costs. You calculate them by dividing each cost item by the year's kilometres, which gives you a price per kilometre. You need this figure when comparing your own vehicle with offers on a freight exchange.

Fuel

Fuel is the largest variable cost. Record your refuelling and the kilometres driven over a month, and you get actual consumption in litres per 100 kilometres. Multiply that by the price per litre and divide by 100, and you have the fuel cost per kilometre. Empty running eats this figure straight out of your margin, because an empty kilometre burns fuel but earns no freight revenue.

Maintenance, tyres and wear parts

Maintenance costs are not spread evenly over the calendar, so they need to be smoothed: take the year's service invoices and the price of tyres relative to their life in kilometres, and divide the total by the kilometres. Add oil, brakes and small wear parts to the same item.

Driver wages and employer contributions

If you employ a driver, wages and employer contributions are mostly fixed, but calculated per kilometre or per hour they tell you what each hour on the road really costs. In Finland, employer contributions (pension, social security and insurance contributions) are typically around 20–25 % of wages. General information on insurance and its terms is available from Finance Finland, the industry association (https://www.finanssiala.fi), but only your own insurer can give you a price.

How do I calculate my own vehicle's monthly cost, step by step?

Step 1: gather the fixed items

Record every item you pay even when the lorry is idle: financing or depreciation, insurance, vehicle tax and fixed-price service contracts. Use the invoices for the past 12 months, not estimates from memory. Divide the annual total by twelve to get the fixed monthly cost.

Step 2: calculate the variable items per kilometre

Multiply fuel consumption (l/100 km) by the price per litre and divide by 100. Do the same for tyres, maintenance and spare parts: the year's costs divided by the year's kilometres. Treat the driver's wage including employer contributions as a fixed monthly item if it is paid regardless of how much the lorry runs.

Step 3: choose the basis of calculation (per kilometre or per hour)

Multiply the variable costs per kilometre by the expected number of kilometres in the month and add the fixed items. The result is the vehicle's actual monthly cost. When comparing freight offers, it is also worth calculating an hourly cost, because loading queues and waiting take up hours but do not show up in kilometres.

The core of the model is this: fixed costs run every day, whether you are running loaded or standing in the yard, so they have to be spread over the kilometres you actually drive. If the total cost is €9,000 a month and the lorry covers 12,000 kilometres, the cost per kilometre is €0.75. This is the figure you compare every freight offer, own customer and backload against. Without it, you can easily sell at a loss and only notice it in the accounts months later.

Checklist: gathering the costs

  • Finance or leasing agreement and interest
  • Insurance invoices (motor third-party liability, comprehensive and carrier's liability insurance)
  • Vehicle tax decision
  • The year's service invoices and spare parts receipts
  • Tyre purchase and fitting costs
  • Fuel card transactions for calculating consumption
  • Payslips and employer contributions
  • Last month's empty kilometres

How do empty running and backloads affect cost?

Running empty costs almost the same as running loaded: fuel is burned, the driver's hours tick on and fixed costs are spread over the empty kilometres too. If a lorry covers 10,000 km a month and 2,000 km of them are empty, a fifth of the total cost is incurred on kilometres that carry no freight. The loaded kilometres then have to earn 25 % more than the bare cost per kilometre to cover costs.

A backload turns the calculation around: when the same costs are spread over more loaded kilometres, the cost per freight kilometre falls. That is why a backload does not need to be expensively priced to be profitable.

Rule of thumb: a drawbar combination or an articulated combination (tractor unit and semi-trailer) typically uses around 30–40 l/100 km, and the heaviest combinations even more, depending on the load and route. You can follow fuel price trends in Statistics Finland's energy price statistics. Multiply the empty kilometres by the cost per kilometre, and you can see the minimum a backload should bring in.

On the RAHTIS load board you can see loads near the delivery point when the vehicle has a job running or coming up: RAHTIS.

Own fleet or subcontracting: which works out cheaper?

The comparison is simple once you have worked out the vehicle's monthly cost. Your own fleet incurs fixed costs every month, even when there are no loads, so an own vehicle only pays off if its utilisation stays high. With subcontracting you pay only the agreed freight rate, but your margin is the difference between your price to the customer and the rate paid to the subcontractor.

In practice, you calculate your own vehicle's cost per kilometre: the monthly cost divided by the expected kilometres. Then you compare it with the subcontractor's price per kilometre. If your own cost is, say, €1.20/km and the subcontractor asks €1.45/km, your own vehicle is cheaper, provided you can also find loads for it on the return leg.

Remember to factor in the chance of a backload. An empty return often makes a job run with your own fleet more expensive than it looks on paper. The same calculation tells you when to hand a job to a subcontractor and when to run it yourself.

How does a cost calculation help you compare freight exchange offers?

When you know your vehicle's cost per hour or per kilometre, assessing a freight exchange offer becomes a calculation, not a guess. Divide the offered rate by all the kilometres the job requires, including the empty ones: if there is no backload, a 500-kilometre job in practice means the cost of 1,000 kilometres for a single freight payment.

Statistics Finland publishes a cost index for road haulage by lorry, which you can use to follow price trends for cost items and to check index clauses in customer contracts. The index shows change, not a euro amount, so you still need your own calculation.

Before accepting an offer, check three things: does the rate cover the fixed costs for the duration of the run, does it cover the route's variable costs in full, and is there a margin left that pays for your own work? If any of the three is not met, it is better to turn the offer down.

How do you keep track of who has paid and who has not?

A paper notebook works until it does not: a signed delivery note goes missing, a transport order stays in someone's inbox, and nobody remembers which job is still unpaid. With several vehicles and jobs coming from different sources, the same information lives in three places.

The principle is simple: one job, one record. The transport order, the driver's sign-off and the invoice sit under the same job, and the payment status is visible in the same place. In RAHTIS, the driver signs off the delivery in the app, an invoice to your own customer is created from the job, and a reminder is sent for an invoice still unpaid after the due date.

The same cost calculation applies here too: if the freight is only paid after 60 days, it ties up cash, and the cost of financing that should be built into your floor price. You see it straight away, not only in the year-end accounts.

FAQ

How much does fuel for a lorry cost per month in Finland?

Work it out from the kilometres: for example, 10,000 km a month at a consumption of 32 l/100 km and a price of €1.75 per litre (example price) comes to about €5,600. In the weightings of Statistics Finland's cost index, fuel accounts for about a fifth of costs, but the share varies with mileage and fuel price. The exact amount depends on consumption, kilometres and the price per litre.

How many kilometres does a lorry need to cover each month to be profitable?

Profitability is not a fixed number of kilometres but a question of utilisation and rate levels. Fixed costs run every month, so the vehicle has to earn enough freight revenue to cover them and the variable costs. For example, 12,000 kilometres a month at a cost of €9,000 means a cost of €0.75 per kilometre, against which every freight rate is compared.

Is the driver's wage included in the vehicle's costs?

Yes. In the cost index weightings, wages including employer contributions account for almost half of the costs of road haulage by lorry. In the calculation, the wage is treated as a fixed monthly item if it is paid regardless of how much the lorry runs, but it is also worth calculating it per hour, so you can see what each hour on the road costs.

How do I calculate the cost per kilometre if the lorry is idle for part of the month?

Divide the month's total cost by the kilometres actually driven. If fixed costs are, say, €3,000 and the lorry only covers 6,000 kilometres because of downtime, the fixed share is €0.50/km, compared with €0.25/km in a full 12,000-kilometre month. That is why empty kilometres and downtime should be recorded, so that comparing freight offers is based on your real cost.

Does RAHTIS cost money, and how does the pricing work?

RAHTIS is priced per vehicle, not per user: the first vehicle is free, and each additional vehicle that has run jobs during the month costs €14.90 a month (excl. VAT). An idle vehicle is free, and no percentage is charged on jobs. The current price list is on the RAHTIS website.
