Key takeaways

  • Work out the cost of running empty per kilometre and set a floor price: a backload paying less than that is not worth taking.
  • Look for a backload before the lorry sets off, not once it is standing empty at the delivery point.
  • The container return deadline, the container depot's opening hours and the driver's remaining driving time constrain the route before you commit to it.
  • Track three figures for each vehicle every month: empty running percentage, cost per empty kilometre and how many return legs were run loaded.
  • Freight exchanges such as Trans.eu and TIMOCOM are sources of backloads; RAHTIS shows loads near the delivery point from its own load board, but does not pull offers from other exchanges.

Planning the return route for an empty trailer or container starts with euros: work out the cost of running empty (fuel, tyres, maintenance, driver time) and compare it with the rate for a backload, minus the cost of any detour and waiting time. This guide gives you a decision model for when to take a backload and when to run empty, plus a checklist for planning the route.

Why does returning an empty trailer or container cost money?

Running empty costs almost as much as running loaded: you burn slightly less fuel, but the driver is still being paid and the vehicle wears just the same. There is simply no revenue. According to Statistics Finland's road freight statistics, in the 2010s around a quarter (23–29 %) of lorry kilometres were driven empty, and that shows up directly in the margin.

Which costs arise even when the lorry is empty

Fuel, driver wages, insurance, tyres, servicing and depreciation all accrue when the lorry is empty too. On top of that there is the opportunity cost: every hour a trailer stands empty waiting to be returned is an hour you could have used for a paid job. The freight revenue disappears; the costs do not.

Why empty running builds up unnoticed in a small firm

When you handle dispatch, invoicing and safety yourself, looking for a backload easily ends up at the bottom of the list. Empty running does not appear on any invoice; it just vanishes into the margin. That is why the first step is to record every empty kilometre, for example in a system such as RAHTIS, where jobs and vehicles are in the same view.

When is a backload worth taking, and when is it not?

The decision is made in euros, not on gut feeling: if the backload rate covers the variable costs and part of the fixed costs, take it. If not, running empty may be the more sensible choice.

The floor price in euros: how to calculate the lowest acceptable rate

First work out the cost of running empty: kilometres × variable cost per kilometre (diesel, tyres, mileage-related maintenance) + driver time. The floor price is that total plus a small margin, say 5–10 %. A backload below the floor does not even cover the cost of the return leg itself, and it takes up time you could spend on a better-paying job.

Three situations where an empty return is the right choice

  1. The backload rate is below the floor and negotiation will not lift it above.
  2. Loading or unloading the backload would delay your own job the next day, which has a better margin.
  3. The container or trailer has its own return deadline, and collecting the load would push the return past it.

How do you plan the return route step by step?

Steps 1–3: the basics before the lorry sets off

Step 1: record where and when the lorry will be empty. Step 2: work out the cost per kilometre of running empty. Fuel and driver wages are the largest cost items in road haulage, so estimate them realistically. Step 3: look for a backload before departure, not once the lorry is standing empty at the delivery point.

Steps 4–5: choosing the backload and fitting the schedule

Step 4: compare backloads on two measures: the rate and the deviation from your route. A load that pays less but heads straight towards home often beats one that pays more but takes the lorry 200 km in the wrong direction. Step 5: fit the loading and unloading times so that they do not put the original job at risk. If the backload's loading slot forces the driver to wait until the next day, the overnight stop eats up the earnings.

Steps 6–7: fixing the route and briefing the driver

Step 6: lock in the route and update the schedule in both directions, so that another job for the customer does not derail the return. Step 7: give the driver clear instructions in a language they understand: loading point, times, how delivery is to be confirmed and whom to call if anything changes.

At this stage a paper notebook is no longer enough. When the job, the consignment note and the invoice are in the same system, nothing has to be copied a second time. In RAHTIS, the job, the driver's sign-off and the invoice to your own customer are in one place, and while the lorry is still on the road you can see loads near the delivery point on the RAHTIS load board. You can set a load alert on saved routes to be notified of suitable new loads.

Which factors affect the choice of return route (distance, timing, container availability)?

The choice of route depends on distance, timing, where the container or trailer is to be returned, the opening hours of the port or container depot, the driver's remaining driving time, fuel costs and the going rate for backloads. Always check the return location, opening hours and driving time before you promise the customer a return.

Port and container depot return times

Empty container returns often fail because the depot or terminal is closed by the time the lorry arrives. Check the opening hours in advance and note them with the route. If the last return time is 16:00 and loading runs late, the return slips to the next day and the lorry stands idle overnight.

Return location and deadline

The return location for an empty container is usually set by the shipping line or its agent, and there is a period of free time for the return, after which detention or demurrage charges start to accrue. Check the return reference, the return location and the last return date before you lock in the route. If you only have one day, picking up a backload on the same route is effectively ruled out and the empty run is already decided.

The driver's driving and rest times

The return route has to be calculated from the driver's remaining driving time, not the distance on the map. If the driver has four hours of driving time left and the return would take six, the route will not fit into the day and the overnight stop eats up the backload revenue. Check the remaining driving time from the tachograph or the driver card data before confirming the route, and record the return on the job so that the driver sees the same schedule as you do.

Fuel price and detours

The price of diesel varies by region and by week, which directly affects whether the detour a backload requires is worth making. If a backload requires an 80 km detour and diesel costs €1.85 a litre, the fuel alone comes to about €44 at a consumption of 30 l/100 km. Add driver time and tyre wear, and the detour easily costs €60–70 or more. If the backload does not bring in more than that, running home empty is cheaper. Update your calculations monthly so that the floor price stays realistic.

How do backloads and freight exchanges help reduce empty running?

On a freight exchange you search for loads that fit your route and timing, and you see who is willing to pay so that your lorry does not run home empty. Exchanges used in the Nordic countries include TIMOCOM, Teleroute and Trans.eu. The principle is the same: you see the offers and the shipper's details before you commit.

Search by route, not by a single town

Do not just look for loads "from Turku"; look along a route that matches your direction. Search by origin and destination region and allow a reasonable detour, say 50–100 km. Remember that driving and rest times limit how long a detour you can make without the return slipping to the next day (Regulation (EC) No 561/2006). Always do the maths: the backload rate minus the fuel and time for the detour, compared with running straight home empty.

Several exchanges side by side

On secondary routes in the Nordic countries, a single exchange has thin coverage, so many small hauliers follow two or three exchanges in parallel. The same load and the same shipper may then appear in several places, and the details are copied by hand into your own system. RAHTIS does not aggregate offers from other exchanges: it shows the loads on its own load board and your own customers' jobs, and you record a job obtained elsewhere yourself.

Checking the shipper's background and payment terms

Before committing to a backload, check out the shipper. The large exchanges usually show a company profile and ratings from other users; also check the business ID (Y-tunnus, the Finnish company registration number) and company details, for example in the Finnish Business Information System YTJ, and, if the relationship continues, the reports required under the Finnish Contractor's Liability Act. If the ratings are poor or the payment period is long, you may end up waiting for your money.

In that case, either agree a shorter payment period in writing or choose another load, even if the rate is slightly lower. For a small firm, a single late invoice can wipe out the entire margin on the backload.

How does software help you manage empty returns?

What to demand from a transport management system for a small firm

When you handle dispatch, invoicing and safety yourself, the job of software is to reduce scraps of paper and separate logins, not add to them. Demand the following: backloads appear in the same view as your own jobs, vehicles and drivers are entered once, the driver's sign-off and the consignment note are linked to the invoice, and you can see who has paid and who has not. Container haulage also needs visibility of return times and deadlines, because return times and detention charges vary by shipping line and by depot.

How do you track and reduce empty running month by month?

Three figures to calculate every month

The first figure is the empty running percentage: empty kilometres divided by all kilometres driven. Calculate it separately for each vehicle, so you can see which route is eating into your margin.

The second figure is the cost per empty kilometre. Take the vehicle's monthly cost (fuel, driver, insurance, depreciation) and divide it by all kilometres driven: an empty kilometre costs in practice the same as a loaded one. Once you know that a kilometre costs, say, €1.20, accepting or rejecting a backload becomes a simple calculation.

The third figure is the load factor on return legs: how many return legs were run loaded.

Once a month, pick three actions: go through the routes of your worst-performing vehicle, ask two regular customers for backloads in advance and check the exchange offers before the lorry becomes free. Record the figures in the same spreadsheet as your unpaid invoices.

Monthly report and spotting trends

Once you have three months of figures, compare them. If the empty running percentage rises from month to month, a route or customer is causing recurring empty running. Find it and either negotiate a fixed-price backload or drop the route. Record every action in the same spreadsheet, so that three months later you can see whether the fix worked. A small firm has no time for heavy analysis, but a ten-minute check is enough to see which way the figures are moving.

FAQ

How much does an empty kilometre cost in Finland?

There is no general figure. Take the vehicle's monthly cost, i.e. fuel, driver wages including employer contributions, insurance, tyres, servicing and depreciation, and divide it by the kilometres driven in the month. The example in this article used €1.20 per kilometre. The figure varies by vehicle and type of operation, so calculate it separately for each vehicle.

Can a container be returned to a different place from where it was collected?

Sometimes, but it has to be agreed in advance with the shipping line or its agent. Ask for the return reference, the permitted return location and the last return date at the same time. Different depots have different opening hours and charging practices, so another return location may make the route work or shift the return by a day. Check the opening hours before you lock in the route.

Should I take a cheap backload or run empty?

Work out the floor price: the cost of running empty plus a small margin, say 5–10 per cent. If the backload rate is below that and negotiation will not lift it, running empty is the right choice. The same applies if loading or unloading would delay a better job the next day, or if the container return deadline would be missed.

How do I find a backload quickly when the lorry is already empty?

Search the freight exchange by origin and destination region, not just by town, and allow a 50–100 km detour. Compare loads on two measures: the rate and the deviation from your route. Deduct the fuel and time for the detour from the rate, and check the driver's remaining driving time so that the return does not slip to the next day.

Can the driver look for a backload on their phone?

Technically yes, as the exchanges run in a browser, but the driver cannot and must not compare offers while driving. A better model is for you to find the load and give the driver clear instructions: loading point, times, how delivery is to be confirmed and a contact for when plans change. When the route and schedule are agreed in advance, there are fewer phone calls.
