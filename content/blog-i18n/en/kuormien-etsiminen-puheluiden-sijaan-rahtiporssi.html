You can find loads on a freight exchange instead of by phone: you upload your availability and route to the platform, browse open loads along your route and bid for them with a single login. This guide compares a day on the phone with a day on the platform side by side, and gives you checklists for the profile, availability and route settings worth sorting out before your first search.

Why does finding loads by phone still take hours?

When you're the dispatcher, the bookkeeper and the drivers' boss all at once, searching by phone eats the hours: you ring brokers one by one, listen to prices, jot jobs down on a scrap of paper and hope someone rings back. The same empty leg you could fill in ten minutes takes half a day.

The hidden costs of phone-based searching in a 1–12 lorry firm

The hidden costs aren't the per-minute call charges but the interrupted work: every call breaks off invoicing, checking CMR notes or planning the next job. The note on a scrap of paper saying "who pays and when" gets forgotten precisely when you need the money most.

In a three-lorry business, a single dispatch run can mean 15–20 calls a day just to fill empty legs. Each call lasts three to five minutes on average, but the real cost is the context switch: when you go back to invoicing after a call, it takes fifteen minutes to get back up to speed. Ten calls a day in practice means an hour and a half of fragmented working time that vanishes from the diary without anyone noticing.

In a one-person business it's worse: when you're driving yourself and making calls during breaks, searching by phone eats into driving time. A break that should be a rest turns into a stressful round of calls with uncertain results. Voicemail is the most common answer, and leaving a call-back request means waiting around, which only adds to fatigue.

Freight exchanges are built for exactly this: Teleroute describes how its service works as shippers publishing loads and hauliers finding them using search criteria, with no phone call (Teleroute). RAHTIS starts from the same problem at the smaller end: you publish the job once and follow it step by step (RAHTIS – Veokorraldus ja veobörs).

How does a freight exchange find a load for your lorry?

A freight exchange works as a two-way marketplace: shippers publish loads, and hauliers find them with filters or by posting their own empty legs. The system matches the two sides automatically when the details fit. The same logic applies in RAHTIS (RAHTIS – Transportledning och lastbörs): a published load is visible to those searching, and you follow the job step by step.

Search filters: origin, destination, date and vehicle type

The search is based on four fields. Origin and destination define your route, the date tells when the lorry is free, and the vehicle type (refrigerated, trailer, container chassis) weeds out loads your vehicle isn't suited to. The more precisely these are filled in on your profile, the fewer matches you get that you have to turn down.

In practice the chain of filters works like this: first you choose the origin area, for example "Helsinki + 150 km". Then you add a destination filter, such as "Tampere or Turku". The third filter is the date: "loading Monday morning, delivery Tuesday". The fourth is the vehicle type: "drop trailer, 13.6 metres, tail lift". The system only shows the loads that pass all four filters. If you leave one field empty, you get dozens of matches that don't suit your vehicle, and you waste time scrolling through them.

Posting an offer vs searching actively

In an active search you browse the loads on offer yourself and submit quotes. By posting your empty leg, you tell the other side where you're heading and when, and the load can come to you. In practice the two complement each other: searching fills the lorry today, and a posted empty leg finds the return load for tomorrow.

What information do you need to prepare before you start searching for loads online?

An exchange search only works first time if your profile, vehicle details and route settings are in order before you open your first request for quotation. An incomplete profile shows up as a bidder nobody picks.

Profile checklist

  • Company details: business ID (Y-tunnus), VAT number, insurance and CMR liability details
  • Vehicles: lorries, trailers, payload, lifting equipment, number of lashing straps
  • Drivers: licence categories, ADR qualification, languages spoken
  • Payment terms and invoicing details ready in advance, so they aren't up for negotiation on every job

Many exchanges show the other party's credit rating and reviews before you quote. Trans.eu's documentation covers, among other things, members' ratings and payment history (Trans.eu Help Center). It's worth gathering the same information about your own customers: in RAHTIS (RAHTIS – Kuljetusten hallinta ja rahtipörssi) you can track not only the job status but also who pays what.

A day on the phone vs a day on the platform: the dispatch workflow side by side

Morning: finding loads and quoting

On a phone day, the morning goes on calls: nobody can see who has a spot load available, so the enquiries go one after another. On a platform day, you open the freight exchange, filter routes and loading times, and see the available loads at a glance. Submitting a quote takes a couple of clicks, and replies arrive as notifications without the phone ringing while you're driving.

Daytime: confirmations, consignment note, driver details

This is where the difference is greatest. In the phone model, the booking, load details and consignment note files live in different places: text messages, email and a scrap of paper. On a platform, the same job appears as a single whole: the driver sees the addresses, times and consignment note directly on their phone, and you follow the status without ringing to ask "where are you?".

When loads, drivers and documents are in the same system, dispatch shifts from chasing information to making decisions: you choose the next load, and the system passes the information on. In RAHTIS, the consignment note, confirmation and status tracking sit in the same view, so in a four-lorry business you no longer leaf through scraps of paper to see who pays and what's still open. The same principle works for a small Nordic business whichever exchange its spot loads come from, and the Latvian-language RAHTIS page (RAHTIS – Transporta vadība un kravu birža) explains how to bring your own customers and exchange jobs together. An honest note: if there aren't enough loads on the platform for a particular route, you'll still have to make calls or check another exchange, so the phone won't disappear entirely.

Evening: invoicing and payment tracking

On a phone day, the evening goes on matching consignment notes with quote notes to raise invoices. On a platform, the end of a job prompts for the POD, and the invoice goes out from the same chain. Payment tracking is visible in the system, so you no longer need a "who owes what" list in a paper notebook.

What are the risks of giving up the phone, and how do you vet the other party?

A phone call is the traditional way to "sniff out" whether a client is reliable, but systematic checks can replace it: online, a fraudster leaves a trail, as long as you note the details down before accepting the job.

Counterparty checklist

Before accepting a load from a freight exchange, check at least: the company's official name and business ID, the client's contact details on a company email address (not a Gmail address), payment history from the exchange's rating points, and whether the client is a verified member. Trans.eu and TIMOCOM both offer members' credit ratings and payment behaviour data for exactly this purpose (Trans.eu credit information service). Keep the list and fill it in for every new client, even if the load is urgent.

Payment terms and advance payments

The biggest risk isn't a wrong address but money: is payment at 30 days or 60, and who pays if the goods arrive but the invoice isn't paid? Agree payment terms in writing before loading, and beware of cash-in-advance requests, which are a common scam. In RAHTIS, the job details, consignment note and invoicing run through the same step-by-step view, so the agreement and documents stay paired (RAHTIS – Transportmanagement und Frachtenbörse).

When is the phone still the better option, even if you use a freight exchange?

The phone beats the platform when time is short. If the lorry is standing empty and a spot load has to be found within the hour, a direct call to a shipper you know or to other hauliers working the area is often the fastest way to get a load moving.

The second situation is an urgent or unusual load. If the loading changes mid-day, a trailer is missing or the delivery time falls through, it's sorted on the phone in seconds. Writing a message and waiting for a reply takes the same minutes.

The third reason is relationships. Your regular customers and most important clients are used to hearing from you, and direct contact maintains trust when spot loads from the freight exchange alone aren't enough to fill the week.

So in practice: the exchange handles searching and paperwork, the phone handles urgency and relationships.

How do you start searching for loads digitally when you have no history on the platform yet?

A new account is a blank sheet, but the first job comes sooner if your profile doesn't look anonymous. Fill in your company details, registration numbers, number of drivers and insurance details straight away, and upload a sample CMR or two from previous deliveries. Respond to your first quotes within a few minutes: on platforms, response time is visible to the other side, and a quick acknowledgement makes up for a missing review history.

Start with small spot loads in your local area, even if the margin is thin. The first successful jobs earn the reviews that open up better-paid routes. Be as careful as you would on the phone: confirm the loading time in writing, ask for a signed consignment note and send the POD straight away.

In small hauliers' experience, it's precisely this combination, load search, POD and invoicing in the same workflow, that makes the switch worthwhile once the paper notebook and separate logins start to become a burden (Reddit discussion of a small haulier's TMS experience).

Which tools for a small haulage business: exchanges and TMS in one package?

For a small business, tools fall into two camps: freight exchanges such as Trans.eu, TIMOCOM, Teleroute, Transportial and Maxmove handle finding loads and filling spot capacity, while a TMS handles dispatch, consignment notes and invoicing. Many run them side by side: the exchange login in one window, a scrap of paper in another, email in a third.

What RAHTIS is suited to and where it falls short

RAHTIS combines both: the shipper publishes the job once and follows the work step by step, while the haulier fills empty legs and keeps the whole fleet in one place. It works from a single lorry upwards, so exchange logins, the notebook and paper CMRs are replaced by a workflow found in a single view.

To be honest, RAHTIS doesn't solve everything. It doesn't replace accounting software or a bank connection, and it doesn't know the loads on every foreign exchange. If you need a wide international load supply or advanced reporting for a larger fleet, a traditional exchange and a bigger TMS may suit you better. RAHTIS is designed for one to twelve lorries.

FAQ

Is using a freight exchange chargeable for a small firm, and how does pricing usually work?

Most exchanges charge a monthly or annual subscription that varies with membership size and features. Some charge separately for each confirmed job. Compare pricing based on your own volumes: for one to three lorries a lighter subscription is often enough, and trial periods help you test whether searching pays off before you commit.

Can I combine several exchanges (e.g. Teleroute, TIMOCOM, Trans.eu) into one search without separate logins?

Usually not directly: the exchanges are separate systems, each with its own login and its own filters. In practice, small hauliers keep one or two exchanges open side by side or use a separate TMS that brings jobs together in one view. RAHTIS meets this need by gathering your own jobs and exchange work under the same workflow.

How is the CMR consignment note handled when the load comes through an exchange?

The consignment note details come with the job information, but signing still takes place at the loading and delivery points. In digital workflows, the driver fills in and signs the CMR on a phone and sends the POD to the system immediately after delivery. A paper version is still an acceptable backup. The most important thing is that the consignment note and the other job details stay in the same chain.

How quickly are loads usually found once a listing is published on the platform?

On popular routes with standard vehicles, offers start coming in within hours, sometimes minutes. With a less common vehicle type or in a remote area, a listing may go days without a match. The quality of the listing directly affects the result: precise availability times, route and vehicle details produce matches faster than a vague listing nobody dares take on.

Do I need a separate TMS, or is a freight exchange account enough?

It depends on how much other work falls outside the exchange. An exchange account handles finding loads, but dispatch, consignment notes, payment tracking and invoicing remain separate tasks. If you run one to twelve lorries and want jobs, documents and invoicing in one view, a TMS adds value. For occasional work with a single lorry, an exchange account may be enough to start with.
