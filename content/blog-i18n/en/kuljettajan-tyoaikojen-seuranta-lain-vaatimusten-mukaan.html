Key takeaways

  • Working time records must be kept until the limitation period for claims ends, i.e. two years from the end of the calendar year in which the right to compensation arose, so keeping them for three years is a safe practice.
  • Tachograph data alone is not enough as working time records; it has to be combined with timesheets and job orders.
  • Responsibility for recording always lies with the employer, even if the recording is done externally or by the driver.
  • Driver card data must be downloaded at the latest every 28 days and vehicle unit data at the latest every 90 days, and the downloaded data must be kept for at least one year.
  • Overtime must be recorded separately during the shift itself, because it feeds directly into the payslip and your readiness for inspection.

Recording drivers' working time in line with legal requirements means logging driving, other work, breaks and periods of availability for each driver in your working time records, in accordance with the Finnish Working Hours Act (872/2019) and EU Directive 2002/15/EC, and keeping those records for at least two years. This guide brings both pieces of legislation together into one checklist, with an example job, including empty running, loading and breaks, logged line by line.

What does the law require when recording drivers' working time?

Recording drivers' working time is based on two pieces of legislation, which set both the limits and the recording obligation. Below is a brief summary of each, and of who is responsible.

The Finnish Working Hours Act 872/2019

The working time of employed drivers is governed by the general Finnish Working Hours Act (872/2019, Finlex), which has its own sections for motor vehicle drivers: daily working time (section 9), the 60-hour weekly cap (section 18), breaks (section 24) and the driver's logbook (section 31). The Act requires the employer to keep records of hours worked, overtime hours and the overtime pay made for them (section 32), and to keep those records until the limitation period for claims ends. The Act came into force at the start of 2020 and replaced the previous Working Hours Act (605/1996). There is no separate drivers' working time act; the working time of self-employed drivers is governed by its own act (349/2013). As the provisions on drivers implement an EU directive, the requirements are in line with other Member States, but the detailed limits may differ.

EU Working Time Directive 2002/15/EC

Directive 2002/15/EC harmonises the working time rules for drivers across the EU. Finnish law was drafted on its basis, so the Directive's requirements are reflected directly in the domestic recording obligation. The Directive sets minimum requirements, such as maximum weekly working time and minimum break lengths, but leaves the details to national legislation. This means that if you run cross-border international traffic, you need to know both Finnish law and the corresponding rules in the destination country.

The employer's and the driver's responsibilities

Responsibility for recording lies with the employer, i.e. you, even if you also do driving work yourself. The driver's job is to report their working time correctly. When recording is in order, a single system holding the data is enough at an inspection, just as RAHTIS brings jobs and their details together in one place. The employer's responsibility also extends to making sure the driver understands their reporting obligation: if the driver doesn't report working time correctly, the penalty still falls on the employer. That is why it's important to give drivers clear instructions and a recording template that minimises human error.

What information must be entered in the working time records?

Mandatory information

For every shift, at least the following must be recorded for drivers' working time records to meet the legal requirements (Working Hours Act 872/2019 and Directive 2002/15/EC):

ItemExample
Driver's name and vehicle registration numberMatti M., ABC-123
Start and finish time of the shift06:00–18:30
Working time and overtime shown separately8 h regular working time, 3 h 45 min overtime
Breaks and their duration45 min break, 11:30–12:15
Periods of availability and standby3 h waiting at the loading point
Time off and daily rest18:30–06:00

Overtime must be recorded separately, because it is paid at an enhanced rate and counts towards the 48-hour maximum working time. Waiting at the loading point is working time when the driver must stay there at the employer's disposal (Working Hours Act, section 3), even if they aren't driving; if they are free to leave the truck, the collective agreement for the truck sector pays the time as waiting-time pay, but it does not count as working time. The records must also show the driver's working time arrangement and any exceptional arrangements that have been applied. For example, general working time (8 hours a day, 40 hours a week) and period-based working time (80 hours per two weeks) are recorded separately, because overtime is calculated differently for each.

Where the data comes from: tachograph, timesheets and other documentation

Tachograph data shows driving, breaks and other work, but it doesn't cover everything. Shift start and finish times, periods of availability and information reported by the driver come from the timesheet or a system entry. In practice you need three sources combined: tachograph printouts, the timesheet and the job orders. Driver card data gives precise times, but it doesn't tell you whether a period was loading, unloading or empty running. The job orders show what the driver was supposed to do, but not always when they did it. The timesheet or system entry is the glue that joins the two together and produces the statutory working time records.

A worked recording example for one shift

Monday: start at 06:00, drop trailer collected at 07:00, 45-minute break at 11:30, consignment note signed at destination at 16:00, load unloaded at 17:30, shift ends at 18:30. What gets recorded: 11 h 45 min working time (a 12.5-hour shift minus the 45-minute break), of which about an hour is waiting at the unloading point. Under general working time, the 3 h 45 min above 8 hours is overtime; under period-based working time, overtime is calculated at the end of the two-week period. When jobs and records run in the same system, as in RAHTIS, the job times and the records sit in the same chain (RAHTIS – Transportledning och lastbörs).

Broken down in more detail, the working day looks like this: 06:00–07:00 is other work: the pre-departure vehicle check, coupling the trailer and checking the freight documents. 07:00–11:30 is driving time, of which the tachograph records 4.5 hours of continuous driving. 11:30–12:15 is the statutory 45-minute break. 12:15–16:00 is driving time, including a short refuelling stop.

16:00–17:30 is unloading and waiting, of which unloading is other work and waiting is working time, because the driver has to stay on site. 17:30–18:30 is the return drive to the employer's yard and vehicle maintenance. This breakdown is what an inspector wants to see.

How long must working time data be kept?

Retention period for working time records

Working time records must be kept until the limitation period for claims ends, i.e. two years from the end of the calendar year in which the right to compensation arose (Working Hours Act, sections 32 and 40). This applies to all recorded data: hours worked, overtime and breaks. The EU Working Time Directive 2002/15/EC requires records to be kept of mobile workers' working time, and those records to be kept for at least two years after the end of the period they cover. In practice it is wise to keep the records for longer than the minimum, because a retrospective claim may come later. For example, a driver may claim overtime pay after the event, and without records you have no evidence of what happened. Keeping records for three years is a safe practice that also covers any late claims.

Retention period for tachograph charts and data

Tachograph printouts and downloaded digital data must be kept for at least one year. If the data is used as the basis for working time records, the same two-year retention obligation applies to it as well. Systematic electronic archiving removes the risk of paper charts going missing. RAHTIS does not archive tachograph files, but you can pass job times from it to other systems via its API (the interface specifications are in the API documentation). Digital data must be kept in its original format, i.e. .ddd and .v1b files must not be edited. If you edit the files, they lose their evidential value at an inspection. That is why it's important to download the card regularly and store the data in a locked archive that only authorised users can access.

What are the limits on working time, overtime and breaks?

Daily and weekly working time

The Working Hours Act (872/2019) sets its own limits for drivers, some of which are stricter than in other sectors. A motor vehicle driver's daily working time may not exceed 11 hours in the 24 hours following daily rest, and it may be extended to no more than 13 hours if the work cannot otherwise be organised (section 9); under the collective agreement for the truck sector, daily working time is limited by the agreement's rules on rest and breaks. Working time covers loading, unloading, maintenance and waiting during which the driver must stay on site, as well as driving. Working time including overtime may average no more than 48 hours a week over four months, and a driver within the scope of the driving and rest time regulation may work no more than 60 hours in any calendar week (section 18). The current Act has no separate annual overtime cap: overtime is limited by the 48-hour average. These limits are worth writing straight into your own checklist, so that recording drivers' working time in line with the law becomes routine rather than a separate project.

Overtime limits and compensation

Overtime is only permitted with the driver's consent and is recorded separately from regular working time. Overtime is paid at an enhanced rate or compensated with time off, so how it's broken down in the records feeds directly into the payslip. Under general working time, overtime is work beyond eight hours a day or 40 hours a week; under period-based working time in the collective agreement for the truck sector, it is work beyond 80 hours in the two-week period. For daily overtime, pay is increased by 50 per cent for the first two hours and by 100 per cent after that; for weekly overtime, by 50 per cent (Working Hours Act, section 20). In period-based working time, the first 12 overtime hours are paid at +50 per cent and the rest at +100 per cent, and the collective agreement may provide for better terms. Record overtime separately during the shift itself and don't leave it to be fixed in the monthly routine, because then the driver doesn't know what their pay is based on.

Breaks and rest periods

Under the driving and rest time regulation 561/2006, a break of at least 45 minutes must be taken after 4.5 hours of driving, and it may be split into two parts (first at least 15 minutes, then at least 30 minutes). Based on working time, the driver must also be given a break of at least 30 minutes for every 5.5 hours of work (Working Hours Act, section 24, and the collective agreement for the truck sector); Directive 2002/15/EC requires a 30-minute break when working time is 6–9 hours and a 45-minute break when it exceeds 9 hours. Daily rest must be at least 11 hours and weekly rest 45 hours. Daily rest may be reduced to nine hours no more than three times between two weekly rest periods. Weekly rest may be reduced to 24 hours, provided at least one regular 45-hour weekly rest is taken in any two consecutive weeks and the reduction is compensated by an equivalent period of rest taken en bloc before the end of the third week following. These exceptions must be recorded separately so that the inspector can see when reduced rest was applied and whether it complied with the rules.

How do you combine tachograph data with working time records?

The tachograph records the hours driven, but working time records need more: working time subject to monitoring, breaks and periods of availability must be derived from the card data and supplemented manually. For driving and rest times, the source data are on the card directly in line with Regulation (EC) No 561/2006, but records meeting the working time legislation only come into being once that data is combined with information on loading and other work. Regulation (EC) No 561/2006 sets driving and rest time limits that are separate from the working time limits. Under the Regulation, continuous driving time is limited to 4.5 hours, and daily driving time to 9 hours, extendable to 10 hours twice a week. These limits apply only to driving, whereas Working Hours Act 872/2019 covers all work, including loading, unloading and waiting.

Downloading the card and the vehicle unit

Download the driver card at the latest every 28 days and the vehicle unit at the latest every 90 days. Import the data into software that reads .ddd and .v1b files directly. RAHTIS does not read tachograph files, but you can bring job times into the same software via the RAHTIS API. That way the card data doesn't stay as PDF printouts but flows straight into your working time records. The card's memory is limited: when it is full, the oldest data is overwritten with new data, and anything not downloaded then has to be retrieved from the vehicle unit, if it is still stored there. That is why card downloads should be set as a calendar reminder, not left to the driver's discretion.

Interpreting anomalies: empty running, loading and waiting

The tachograph doesn't distinguish empty running from laden running, or loading from other work. Mark them yourself: empty running as driving, loading as other work, and waiting as either other work or availability, depending on whether the driver is free to use the time as they wish. Without a note, unclear periods are hard to explain at an inspection. For example, if the driver spends two hours at the loading point, the tachograph will show "other work", "availability" or "break/rest" depending on which activity the driver selected. If the driver selected break but was actually loading, the working time records end up wrong. Correct these anomalies in the monthly routine by comparing job orders with the card data.

Monthly routine for a small firm

Go through drivers' cards every month: compare the hours on the card with the records, correct anomalies and save everything before the start of the following month. The monthly routine is when you notice if a driver has recorded breaks that weren't taken, or if overtime is missing. Allow 30 minutes per driver for the routine. If there are many anomalies, retrain the driver on how to record. Don't leave corrections until the following month, because then errors pile up and become harder to fix.

What happens if recording is inadequate?

Inspections and authorities

Compliance with the Working Hours Act is supervised by the occupational safety and health authority, which since the start of 2026 has been the Finnish Licensing and Supervision Agency (Lupa- ja valvontavirasto; previously the occupational safety and health divisions of the Regional State Administrative Agencies, AVI); the police also check driving and rest times at roadside checks. At an inspection you may be asked for working time records, tachograph data and employment contracts. If records are missing or incomplete, that in itself is a breach of the law, even if the driver's working hours were otherwise within the rules. An inspection can lead to written notices or obligations with deadlines, and the matter may end up being put right afterwards by an employers' association or a lawyer, at greater cost. An occupational safety and health inspection may be announced in advance or unannounced. Unannounced inspections often target firms whose operations have attracted attention, for example through lots of overtime or short rest periods. That is why records must always be up to date, not just when an inspection looms.

Duty to correct and the driver's rights

Inadequate recording often ends up costing the driver: overtime, days off and rest periods aren't realised if they aren't recorded. The driver can claim compensation retrospectively within two years of the end of the calendar year in which the right arose (Working Hours Act, section 40), so a claim may cover periods almost three years in the past. Correct the records before an inspection, not during it. If a driver claims compensation after the event and you have no records showing that no overtime was worked, the burden of proof shifts to you. This means you may have to pay compensation even if the overtime was never actually worked. That is why the records protect both driver and employer.

How should working time recording be organised in a 1–12 vehicle firm?

Options: forms, spreadsheet and software

In a one-vehicle firm, a paper form or freely structured records will do, as long as the details for every 24-hour period can be found. From two vehicles upwards, paper starts to leak: shifts, tachograph printouts and allowances payable are recorded in different places, and a missing entry at an inspection is your responsibility. Remember too that tachograph data must be kept for at least one year under Regulation (EU) No 165/2014. A spreadsheet is enough if you record with discipline, but it won't remind you of missing entries or total up overtime for you. At three vehicles a spreadsheet becomes prone to human error: one forgotten overtime entry a month per driver adds up to 36 entries a year, and they're missing at the inspection.

Selection criteria, and RAHTIS measured against them

Choose a tool that meets three conditions: it records start and finish times, calculates the overtime limits for you and passes data to the finance side without re-keying. In RAHTIS, job entries, driver shifts and documents are in the same place, which reduces the number of loose forms. To be honest, RAHTIS is not a comprehensive HR system covering Working Hours Act 872/2019, nor does it replace tachograph data as the official source. When evaluating software, check three things: does it support the breakdown requirements of Finnish working time law, does it read tachograph file formats directly, and does it export to payroll? If all three are met, you'll save time every month.

Where RAHTIS doesn't help (yet)

Actual payroll, annual leave records and analysis of tachograph files are currently handled by other tools. RAHTIS's strength is managing jobs and transport, not serving as the final archive for statutory working time records. So combine RAHTIS entries with either a spreadsheet or a payroll program where the official records live. For example, you can export the hours from the RAHTIS driver report as a CSV or Excel file and import them into your payroll program, where overtime and compensation are finally calculated. This two-system model is common in small firms, and it works as long as the transfer is documented and repeatable.

Step by step: a checklist for setting up statutory recording

Steps 1–7

  1. Establish which rules apply to your company: employed drivers are covered by the Working Hours Act (872/2019) and the sector's collective agreement, self-employed drivers by Act 349/2013, and those driving within the scope of the driving and rest time regulation also by Regulation 561/2006.
  2. Decide how you will record: a spreadsheet, off-the-shelf working time software, or recording built into a system such as RAHTIS.
  3. Set the contractual working time for each driver and write it down.
  4. Introduce a recording template that captures working time, overtime, breaks and hours attracting pay supplements every day.
  5. Reconcile tachograph card data with the records at least once a week.
  6. Set the retention period to at least the limitation period, i.e. two years from the end of the calendar year in which the data was created.
  7. Review the records every month and correct gaps straight away.

Monthly recurring routine

Block out a fixed slot in the calendar: check hours exceeding the limits, sign off any corrections with the drivers and back up the data. The same routine every month keeps you ready for inspection without a separate project. Also review the rest periods drivers actually took: was the 45-hour weekly rest taken, and did the 11-hour daily rest comply with the rules? If rest was reduced, check whether the reduction was permitted and whether compensating rest was given. Record these checks as part of the routine so you have evidence of regular monitoring if an inspector asks for it.

FAQ

Can a tachograph printout be used as working time records on its own?

No. Tachograph data covers driving, breaks and other work, but not shift start and finish times, periods of availability or information reported by the driver. Records only come into being when card data is combined with timesheets and job orders. Unclear periods, such as empty running or waiting at the loading point, must be marked separately so they can be explained at an inspection.

What do I do if a driver forgets to record overtime or breaks?

Correct the gaps straight away in the monthly routine by comparing the card hours with the records and signing off the corrections with the driver. Missing entries can cost you in retrospective overtime pay or penalties. Responsibility for the records always lies with the employer, so introduce a fixed weekly routine in which card data and records are reconciled before the month ends.

Do the working time rules also apply to owner-drivers and light goods transport?

An owner-driver's working time is governed by the Act on the Working Time of Self-Employed Drivers in Road Transport (349/2013): working time may average no more than 48 hours a week and may not exceed 60 hours in any single week, there are separate rules on breaks and night work, and the owner-driver must have working time records or other reliable evidence covering the previous two years. The driving and rest time regulation applies to owner-drivers in the same way as to employed drivers. For light goods transport and where the line for commercial operation lies, check the legislation itself.

Do paper CMR consignment notes and tachograph charts have to be kept for the same length of time?

No. Tachograph charts and downloaded digital data must be kept for at least one year, but if the data is used as the basis for working time records, the two-year retention obligation applies to it too. Working time records must be kept until the limitation period for claims ends, i.e. two years from the end of the calendar year in which the right to compensation arose. The CMR Convention sets no retention period, but where a consignment note is an accounting voucher or an invoice attachment, the Finnish Accounting Act (1336/1997) requires it to be kept for six years from the end of the year in which the financial year ended, so in practice it has the longest retention period of these three types of document.

Can I make a subcontractor responsible for recording working time?

Not entirely. Responsibility for recording lies with the employer, and a missing entry at an inspection is your responsibility, even if the recording is done externally. A subcontractor or software can do the practical work, but the obligations, such as two-year retention and inspection readiness, stay with you. That is why an outsourced service should be documented and the data it produces checked regularly.
