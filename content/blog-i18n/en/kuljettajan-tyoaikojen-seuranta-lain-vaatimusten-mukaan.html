Key takeaways

  • Working time records must be kept for at least two years, but keeping them for three years also covers late compensation claims.

Recording drivers' working time in line with legal requirements means logging driving, other work, breaks and periods of availability for each driver in your working time records, in accordance with the Finnish working time legislation for drivers (445/2019) and EU Directive 2002/15/EC, and keeping those records for at least two years. This guide brings both pieces of legislation together into one checklist, with an example job, including empty running, loading and breaks, logged line by line.

What does the law require when recording drivers' working time?

Recording drivers' working time is based on two pieces of legislation, which set both the limits and the recording obligation. Below is a brief summary of each, and of who is responsible.

The Finnish Drivers' Working Time Act 445/2019

The Finnish Act on the working and rest times of vehicle drivers (445/2019, Finlex) applies to drivers in commercial road transport. It requires the employer to keep working time records showing hours worked, overtime and breaks. The same Act sets the retention periods and the maximum working time limits. The Act came into force in 2020 and replaced the previous drivers' working time act. As the Act is Finland's national implementation of an EU directive, its requirements are in line with other Member States, but the detailed maximum limits may differ.

EU Working Time Directive 2002/15/EC

Directive 2002/15/EC harmonises the working time rules for drivers across the EU. Finnish law was drafted on its basis, so the Directive's requirements are reflected directly in the domestic recording obligation. The Directive sets minimum requirements, such as maximum weekly working time and minimum rest, but leaves the details to national legislation. This means that if you run cross-border international traffic, you need to know both Finnish law and the corresponding rules in the destination country.

The employer's and the driver's responsibilities

Responsibility for recording lies with the employer, i.e. you, even if you also do driving work yourself. The driver's job is to report their working time correctly. When recording is in order, a single system holding the data is enough at an inspection, just as RAHTIS brings jobs and their details together in one place. The employer's responsibility also extends to making sure the driver understands their reporting obligation: if the driver doesn't report working time correctly, the penalty still falls on the employer. That is why it's important to give drivers clear instructions and a recording template that minimises human error.

What information must be entered in the working time records?

Mandatory information

For every shift, at least the following must be recorded for drivers' working time records to meet the legal requirements (Act 445/2019 and Directive 2002/15/EC):

Breaks and their duration45 min break, 11:30–12:15
Periods of availability and standby3 h waiting at the loading point
Time off and job alternation leave18:30–06:00

Overtime must be recorded separately, because it has its own maximum limits and must be compensated. Periods of availability, such as waiting at the loading point, count as work even when the driver isn't driving. The records must also show the driver's working time category and any exceptional arrangements that have been applied. For example, regular working time and split working time are recorded separately because their overtime thresholds are calculated differently.

Where the data comes from: tachograph, timesheets and other documentation

Tachograph data shows driving, breaks and other work, but it doesn't cover everything. Shift start and finish times, periods of availability and information reported by the driver come from the timesheet or a system entry. In practice you need three sources combined: tachograph printouts, the timesheet and the job orders. Driver card data gives precise times, but it doesn't tell you whether a period was loading, unloading or empty running. The job orders show what the driver was supposed to do, but not always when they did it. The timesheet or system entry is the glue that joins the two together and produces the statutory working time records.

A worked recording example for one shift

Monday: start at 06:00, drop trailer collected at 07:00, 45-minute break at 11:30, consignment note signed at destination at 16:00, return load unloaded at 17:30, shift ends at 18:30. What gets recorded: 9 h working time, 2 h overtime, 45 min break and 1 h of availability at the loading point. When jobs and records run in the same system, as in RAHTIS, the job times and the records sit in the same chain (RAHTIS – Transportledning och lastbörs).

Broken down in more detail, the working day looks like this: 06:00–07:00 is other work, covering vehicle checks and driving to the collection. 07:00–11:30 is driving time, of which the tachograph records 4.5 hours of continuous driving. 11:30–12:15 is the statutory 45-minute break. 12:15–16:00 is driving time, including a short refuelling stop.

16:00–17:30 is unloading and waiting, of which unloading is other work and waiting is availability. 17:30–18:30 is the return drive to the employer's yard and vehicle maintenance. This breakdown is what an inspector wants to see.

How long must working time data be kept?

Retention period for working time records

Working time records must be kept for at least two years. This applies to all recorded data: hours worked, overtime and breaks. The EU Working Time Directive 2002/15/EC requires Member States to ensure that employers keep records of overtime and make them available to the enforcement authorities. In practice it is wise to keep the records for longer than the minimum, because a retrospective claim may come later. For example, a driver may claim overtime pay after the event, and without records you have no evidence of what happened. Keeping records for three years is a safe practice that also covers any late claims.

Retention period for tachograph charts and data

Tachograph printouts and downloaded digital data must be kept for at least one year. If the data is used as the basis for working time records, the same two-year retention obligation applies to it as well. Storing data systematically, for example in a RAHTIS-type system whose interface specifications are in the API documentation, removes the risk of paper charts going missing. Digital data must be kept in its original format, i.e. .ddd and .v1b files must not be edited. If you edit the files, they lose their evidential value at an inspection. That is why it's important to download the card regularly and store the data in a locked archive that only authorised users can access.

What are the limits on working time, overtime and breaks?

Daily and weekly working time

The Finnish Drivers' Working Time Act (445/2019) sets its own limits, some of which are stricter than in other sectors. Daily working time may not exceed 15 hours, and this covers loading, unloading, maintenance and waiting as well as driving. Weekly working time is 40 hours on average, calculated over a four-month reference period, so an individual week can be longer as long as the average stays within the limit. Under the general working time rules a week may not exceed 48 hours, and no more than 180 hours of overtime may accrue in a calendar year. These limits are worth writing straight into your own checklist, so that recording drivers' working time in line with the law becomes routine rather than a separate project.

Overtime limits and compensation

Overtime is only permitted with the driver's consent and is recorded separately from regular working time. Overtime is paid at an enhanced rate or compensated with time off, so how it's broken down in the records feeds directly into the payslip. The overtime threshold for regular working time is eight hours a day or 40 hours a week. For split working time the overtime threshold is nine hours a day. Overtime pay is at least 50 per cent of the hourly wage, but the collective agreement may provide for a higher rate. Record overtime separately during the shift itself and don't leave it to be fixed in the monthly routine, because then the driver doesn't know what their pay is based on.

Breaks and rest periods

A working day of more than six hours must include a break of at least 45 minutes, which may be split into two parts (at least 15 and 30 minutes). Daily rest must be at least 11 hours and weekly rest 45 hours. Daily rest may be reduced to nine hours three times a week if the driver returns home or to a place designated by the employer. Weekly rest may be reduced to 36 hours, but compensating rest must then be given the following week. These exceptions must be recorded separately so that the inspector can see when reduced rest was applied and whether it complied with the rules.

How do you combine tachograph data with working time records?

The tachograph records the hours driven, but working time records need more: working time subject to monitoring, breaks and periods of availability must be derived from the card data and supplemented manually. For driving and rest times, the source data are on the card directly in line with Regulation (EC) No 561/2006, but records meeting the working time legislation only come into being once that data is combined with information on loading and other work. Regulation (EC) No 561/2006 sets driving and rest time limits that are separate from the working time limits. Under the Regulation, continuous driving time is limited to 4.5 hours, and daily driving time to 9 hours, extendable to 10 hours twice a week. These limits apply only to driving, whereas Act 445/2019 covers all work, including loading, unloading and waiting.

Downloading the card and the vehicle unit

Download the driver card at least every 28 days and the vehicle unit at least every 90 days. Import the data into software that reads .v1b and .ddd files directly, such as via the RAHTIS API. That way the card data doesn't stay as PDF printouts but flows straight into your working time records. If you don't download the card within 28 days, it starts overwriting the oldest data with newer data, and you lose information for good. That is why card downloads should be set as a calendar reminder, not left to the driver's discretion.

Interpreting anomalies: empty running, loading and waiting

The tachograph doesn't distinguish empty running or loading. Mark them by hand: empty running at the start of the drive, loading as the start of work, waiting as a period of availability. Without a note, the inspector will treat unclear periods as working time, which costs you. For example, if the driver spends two hours at the loading point, the tachograph will show either "other work" or "break" depending on what the driver selected. If the driver selected break but was actually loading, the working time records end up wrong. Correct these anomalies in the monthly routine by comparing job orders with the card data.

Monthly routine for a small firm

Go through drivers' cards every month: compare the hours on the card with the records, correct anomalies and save everything before the start of the following month. The monthly routine is when you notice if a driver has recorded breaks that weren't taken, or if overtime is missing. Allow 30 minutes per driver for the routine. If there are many anomalies, retrain the driver on how to record. Don't leave corrections until the following month, because then errors pile up and become harder to fix.

What happens if recording is inadequate?

Inspections and authorities

In Finland, occupational safety and health enforcement is the responsibility of the Regional State Administrative Agencies (AVI), and at an inspection you may be asked for working time records, tachograph data and employment contracts. If records are missing or incomplete, that in itself is a breach of the law, even if the driver's working hours were otherwise within the rules. An inspection can lead to written notices or obligations with deadlines, and the matter may end up being put right afterwards by an employers' association or a lawyer, at greater cost. An AVI inspection may be announced in advance or unannounced. Unannounced inspections often target firms whose operations have attracted attention, for example through lots of overtime or short rest periods. That is why records must always be up to date, not just when an inspection looms.

Duty to correct and the driver's rights

Inadequate recording often ends up costing the driver: overtime, days off and rest periods aren't realised if they aren't recorded. The driver has the right to claim for working time retrospectively within a reasonable period, and a claim may even be made for periods years in the past. Correct the records before an inspection, not during it. If a driver claims compensation after the event and you have no records showing that no overtime was worked, the burden of proof shifts to you. This means you may have to pay compensation even if the overtime was never actually worked. That is why the records protect both driver and employer.

How should working time recording be organised in a 1–12 vehicle firm?

Options: forms, spreadsheet and software

In a one-vehicle firm, a paper form or freely structured records will do, as long as the details for every 24-hour period can be found. From two vehicles upwards, paper starts to leak: shifts, tachograph printouts and allowances payable are recorded in different places, and a missing entry at an inspection is your responsibility. Remember too that tachograph data must be kept for at least one year under Regulation (EU) No 165/2014. A spreadsheet is enough if you record with discipline, but it won't remind you of missing entries or total up overtime for you. At three vehicles a spreadsheet becomes prone to human error: one forgotten overtime entry a month per driver adds up to 36 entries a year, and they're missing at the inspection.

Selection criteria, and RAHTIS measured against them

Choose a tool that meets three conditions: it records start and finish times, calculates the overtime limits for you and passes data to the finance side without re-keying. In RAHTIS, job entries, driver shifts and documents are in the same place, which reduces the number of loose forms. To be honest, RAHTIS is not a comprehensive HR system covering Act 445/2019, nor does it replace tachograph data as the official source. When evaluating software, check three things: does it support the breakdown requirements of Finnish working time law, does it read tachograph file formats directly, and does it export to payroll? If all three are met, you'll save time every month.

Where RAHTIS doesn't help (yet)

Payroll export, annual leave records and automatic analysis of tachograph files are currently handled by other tools. RAHTIS's strength is managing jobs and transport, not serving as the final archive for statutory working time records. So combine RAHTIS entries with either a spreadsheet or a payroll program where the official records live. For example, you can export job details from RAHTIS as a CSV file and import them into your payroll program, where overtime and compensation are finally calculated. This two-system model is common in small firms, and it works as long as the transfer is documented and repeatable.

Step by step: a checklist for setting up statutory recording

Steps 1–7

  1. Establish whether your company is covered by the Finnish Drivers' Working Time Act (445/2019) or only by EU Working Time Directive 2002/15/EC – most 1–12 vehicle firms are covered by both.
  2. Decide how you will record: a spreadsheet, off-the-shelf working time software, or recording built into a system such as RAHTIS.
  3. Set the contractual working time for each driver and write it down.
  4. Introduce a recording template that captures working time, overtime, breaks and hours attracting pay supplements every day.
  5. Reconcile tachograph card data with the records at least once a week.
  6. Set the retention period to at least the two years required by law.
  7. Review the records every month and correct gaps straight away.

Monthly recurring routine

Block out a fixed slot in the calendar: check hours exceeding the limits, sign off any corrections with the drivers and back up the data. The same routine every month keeps you ready for inspection without a separate project. Also review the rest periods drivers actually took: was the 45-hour weekly rest taken, and did the 11-hour daily rest comply with the rules? If rest was reduced, check whether the reduction was permitted and whether compensating rest was given. Record these checks as part of the routine so you have evidence of regular monitoring if an inspector asks for it.

FAQ

Can a tachograph printout be used as working time records on its own?

No. Tachograph data covers driving, breaks and other work, but not shift start and finish times, periods of availability or information reported by the driver. Records only come into being when card data is combined with timesheets and job orders. Unclear periods, such as empty running or waiting at the loading point, must be marked separately so the inspector doesn't treat them as working time.

What do I do if a driver forgets to record overtime or breaks?

Correct the gaps straight away in the monthly routine by comparing the card hours with the records and signing off the corrections with the driver. Without an entry, the inspector will treat unclear periods as working time, which costs you in overtime pay or fines. Responsibility for the records always lies with the employer, so introduce a fixed weekly routine in which card data and records are reconciled before the month ends.

Do the working time rules also apply to owner-drivers and light goods transport?

The Finnish Act on the working and rest times of vehicle drivers (445/2019) applies to commercial road transport, so an owner-driver's own driving may also fall within its scope. For light goods transport and where the line for commercial operation lies, check the legislation itself. If your operations fall within the Act, the recording and retention obligations are the same as for employed drivers.

Do paper CMR consignment notes and tachograph charts have to be kept for the same length of time?

No. Tachograph charts and downloaded digital data must be kept for at least one year, but if the data is used as the basis for working time records, the two-year retention obligation applies to it too. Working time records must be kept for at least two years. Paper CMR consignment notes must be kept for at least five years under the international road transport convention, so they have the longest retention period of these three types of document.

Can I make a subcontractor responsible for recording working time?

Not entirely. Responsibility for recording lies with the employer, and a missing entry at an inspection is your responsibility, even if the recording is done externally. A subcontractor or software can do the practical work, but the obligations, such as two-year retention and inspection readiness, stay with you. That is why an outsourced service should be documented and the data it produces checked regularly.
