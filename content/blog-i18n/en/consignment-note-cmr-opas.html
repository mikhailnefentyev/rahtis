The CMR consignment note is the document for international road carriage that evidences the contract of carriage, the taking over of the goods and the terms of carriage, and its entries decide the division of liability between the sender, the carrier and the consignee. This guide covers the legal basis of the CMR Convention, the numbered boxes 1–24 of the most common form layout one by one, and what each party's signature means in law.

What is a CMR consignment note and why is it mandatory?

The CMR consignment note is an international document for the carriage of goods that proves the carrier has taken over the goods and that a contract of carriage exists. At the same time it is the proof of delivery on which you invoice the customer.

The CMR Convention and Finland

The 1956 CMR Convention has bound Finland since 1973, as well as Sweden, Norway and Denmark; it defines the content of the consignment note and the parties' liabilities in the official text of the CMR Convention (UNECE). When you carry goods for reward across a border and the country of loading or delivery is a contracting state, the CMR applies: the contract is confirmed by a consignment note, and its entries and signatures determine who bears liability for damage and delay.

When a CMR consignment note is not required

For purely domestic journeys the CMR is not mandatory: a domestic consignment note under the Finnish Road Transport Contracts Act or another delivery document is enough. On international journeys an electronic e-CMR can replace paper when both the country of loading and the country of delivery have joined its Additional Protocol; Finland ratified the Protocol in 2019. In RAHTIS the signature and the scanned consignment note are saved on the job, and data protection is described in the RAHTIS privacy notice.

Who fills it in, who signs it and who is liable?

The sender's responsibility

The sender fills in the key details of the consignment note: the parties, a description of the goods, the weight and the delivery address. By signing, the sender confirms that the information given is correct and is liable for any errors in it. If the details of the goods are incomplete, the sender bears the consequences.

The carrier taking over the goods

The driver's signature means the carrier has taken over the goods and checked what is apparent: the number of packages, their marks and numbers, and the apparent condition of the goods and packaging. Hidden defects cannot be detected, and under Article 17 of the CMR the carrier can be relieved of liability for damage caused by the inherent nature of the goods or defective packing. When you collect a load, check the boxes before you sign, because your signature binds you to what the note says.

The consignee's signature and the POD

The consignee's signature is the proof of delivery (POD): it proves the goods have been delivered and accepted. Without it, invoicing stays open and you may be left waiting weeks for your money. The RAHTIS driver app takes the consignee's signature on screen and scans the consignment note onto the job, so you know immediately which delivery is complete and which needs a phone call (RAHTIS API documentation).

CMR form boxes 1–12: the parties and the goods

The top half of the form shows who is sending, who is receiving, where the goods travel from and to, and what was loaded onto the vehicle. These boxes determine who can bring a claim and to whom the proof of delivery belongs. The numbers follow the most common form layout; some layouts number the boxes differently.

Boxes 1–4: sender, consignee and right of disposal

Box 1 records the sender and box 2 the consignee. Box 3 is the place of delivery of the goods, and box 4 the place and date of taking over the goods, i.e. loading. The right of disposal is not a box on the form: under Article 12 of the CMR the sender may change, for example, the place of delivery or the consignee until the second copy of the consignment note is handed to the consignee, unless the sender has stated on the note that the consignee holds this right from the start.

Boxes 5–12: attachments and details of the goods

Box 5 records the documents attached to the consignment note, such as customs documents. Boxes 6–10 describe the goods: marks and numbers, number of packages, method of packing, nature of the goods and statistical number. Box 11 is the gross weight in kilograms and box 12 the volume in cubic metres. The electronic CMR holds the same particulars, and the UNECE e-CMR guide explains how the Additional Protocol works; the countries that have joined it include Finland, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Estonia, Germany and Poland.

Why the top half deserves care: box 3 says where the goods are to be delivered, and if the sender changes the place of delivery during the journey, ask for the change in writing. Boxes 6–12 serve as evidence of quantity and condition at the time of taking over, so a missing number of packages or gross weight shifts the dispute onto the carrier. Box 5 ties the customs paperwork to the consignment note: under Article 11 of the CMR the sender is liable for missing or inadequate documents, but an attachment that isn't listed makes that harder to show. In RAHTIS the order details are the same for the driver, the shipper and the API, and you can explore the API in the Finnish-language RAHTIS API documentation.

CMR form boxes 13–24: instructions, charges, reservations and signatures

Boxes 13–24 cover the sender's instructions, payment terms, the carrier's details, reservations and signatures. They are short, but this is where most after-the-event disputes arise, because the boxes are often filled in in a hurry at the loading bay.

Boxes 13–15: instructions, payment and cash on delivery

Box 13 holds the sender's instructions, such as customs and other formalities and handling instructions like temperature requirements. Box 14 is the instruction for payment of carriage (carriage paid or carriage forward), and box 15 is cash on delivery, the amount to be collected from the consignee before the goods are handed over. If the cash on delivery is not collected, the carrier is liable to the sender for it (Article 21). If carriage is invoiced later, state the payment instruction clearly so that the driver and invoicing have the same information.

Boxes 16–24: carrier, reservations and signatures

Box 16 records the carrier and box 17 any successive carriers. Box 18 holds the carrier's reservations and observations on taking over the goods, box 19 special agreements (such as a declared value or an agreed delivery time), and box 20 the amounts to be paid: carriage, supplementary charges, customs duties and other charges, and who pays them. Box 21 is the place and date the note was made out, boxes 22 and 23 are the signatures of the sender and the carrier, and box 24 is the consignee's receipt for the goods.

Paper or electronic CMR consignment note?

Both paper and electronic consignment notes are legal. The electronic version rests on the 2008 Additional Protocol to the CMR Convention, which Finland ratified in 2019, and it can be used on carriage between countries that have joined the Protocol.

The legal status of e-CMR

The electronic consignment note (e-CMR) is made out under the Additional Protocol to the CMR Convention, and its electronic signature has the same legal effect as one on paper, provided the system guarantees the authenticity and integrity of the document. The problem isn't the law but the fact that the loader or consignee may not be on an e-CMR system.

What works in practice in the Nordic countries

For a small fleet, paper still works: the driver gets a signed copy, scans it and sends it to the office. It's slow, and paper gets lost. If your customers are subcontractors found through TIMOCOM or Trans.eu, ask in advance whether they accept e-CMR. RAHTIS saves the signature and the scanned consignment note on the same job for invoicing, but honestly: if the consignee insists on pen and paper, going electronic only gets you halfway.

What happens if the CMR consignment note contains an error or is missing?

A missing or incorrect CMR consignment note does not invalidate the contract of carriage, but it shifts the burden of proof onto you: if the goods are damaged, you have to show what you delivered and in what condition. In practice, missing paperwork shows up as late invoices and disputes over whether the goods arrived at all.

The most common mistakes on small hauliers' forms

Box 18 (carrier's reservations) is left blank even though the packaging was poor or the seal was broken. The consignee records the damage next to its receipt in box 24, and without a reservation of your own you carry the risk alone. Other typical ones: the description of the goods is written solely on the sender's say-so, package details are vague ("one load"), and box 20 is left blank, so surcharges go uninvoiced.

How liability limits relate to insurance

The liability limit of the CMR Convention (8.33 SDR per kilogram of gross weight of the goods lost or damaged, Article 23) does not replace your insurance, but the insurer assesses the claim precisely on the basis of what is written on the consignment note. A correctly completed form is the cheapest insurance evidence you will ever get.

How does the CMR consignment note fit into day-to-day driving and invoicing?

When you drive and invoice yourself, the consignment note is three documents in one: evidence of the contract of carriage, the proof of delivery and an attachment to the invoice. The structure of the form supports this: boxes 1–15 give the parties, the goods and the instructions, boxes 16–20 the carrier, reservations and charges, and boxes 21–24 the signatures. The 2007 model in the IRU's guidance on the CMR form numbers the boxes differently (reservations, for example, are in box 8), so check which layout you are using. Without the consignee's receipt in box 24 you have a journey, but no proof of delivery.

POD, rate confirmation and invoice in one chain

In practice, the POD, rate confirmation and invoice form a chain: the rate confirmation shows the price, the consignment note shows what was carried and box 18 the carrier's reservations, and the consignee's signature releases the invoice. When these three documents are together, the paper "who owes what" notebook becomes unnecessary. Clear handwriting pays: an illegible signature or a missing date is the most common reason for late payment.

Subcontracting from a freight exchange: whose CMR?

When you take a load from TIMOCOM, Trans.eu or Teleroute, the client usually provides its own consignment note on which you are entered as the carrier. Check box 16: your company must appear there if you want your own liability and your own invoice for the freight exchange booking. Keep a copy yourself: if the client disputes the delivery, your signature on the consignment note is your only evidence.

FAQ

Can the sender or a broker fill in the CMR consignment note instead of the carrier?

Yes. The sender fills in the key details of the consignment note and is responsible for their accuracy. A broker or client may also provide a ready-made consignment note on which you are entered as the carrier. Your signature covers what you were able to check: check the number of packages, their marks and condition before signing and record any shortcomings as reservations, because your signature binds you to what the note says.

What is box 13 (sender's instructions) on the CMR and when is it mandatory?

Box 13 is reserved for the sender's instructions: customs and other formalities, and instructions on handling the goods during carriage. It isn't mandatory for every journey, but if the carriage involves customs formalities or the goods need special handling, such as temperature requirements or a specific unloading sequence, the instructions must be on paper. Verbal instructions don't appear on the consignment note, so an unrecorded instruction shifts the risk of a dispute onto you.

How many originals and copies of the CMR form are needed?

Under the CMR Convention the consignment note is made out in three original copies: the first goes to the sender, the second accompanies the goods to the consignee and the third is kept by the carrier. Form sets often include an extra copy for other purposes. For a small haulier, the most important ones are the copy that travels with you and the proof of delivery signed by the consignee. Keep a copy yourself, because if the client disputes the delivery, the signed consignment note is your only evidence.

Can a CMR consignment note be corrected afterwards if I spot a mistake?

A mistake can be corrected, but the correction must be agreed by all parties and be traceable. A private amendment made in pen without signatures loses its evidential value in a dispute. If you spot the mistake before signing, correct it immediately. If the consignee has already recorded reservations, add your own remark and ask for it to be confirmed.

Is a photographed POD without a signed CMR acceptable for invoicing?

A photo can support an invoice, but it does not replace a signed proof of delivery. Without the consignee's signature, invoicing stays open and the burden of proof shifts to you: you have to show what you delivered and in what condition. Best practice is to scan the signed paper and attach it to the invoice together with the photos.
