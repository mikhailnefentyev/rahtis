The CMR consignment note is the legally binding document for international road carriage; it proves that the goods were taken over and sets out the terms of carriage and the division of liability between the sender, the carrier and the consignee. This guide covers the legal basis of the CMR Convention, all 21 boxes of the standard form one by one, and what each party's signature means in law.

What is a CMR consignment note and why is it mandatory?

The CMR consignment note is an international document for the carriage of goods that proves the carrier has taken over the goods and that a contract of carriage exists. At the same time it is the proof of delivery on which you invoice the customer and look for a return load.

The CMR Convention and Finland

The 1956 CMR Convention is binding on Finland and all the Nordic countries; it defines the content of the form and the parties' liabilities in the official text of the CMR Convention (UNECE). When you carry goods across a border, a consignment note is almost always required, and its signatures determine who bears liability for damage and delay.

When a CMR consignment note is not required

For purely domestic journeys, a domestic consignment note or other delivery document is often sufficient. The electronic e-CMR is also a permitted alternative to paper in Finland. RAHTIS handles the consignment note and proof of delivery chain digitally, and data protection is described in the RAHTIS privacy notice.

Who fills it in, who signs it and who is liable?

The sender's responsibility

The sender fills in the key details of the consignment note: the parties, a description of the goods, the weight and the delivery address. By signing, the sender confirms that the information given is correct and is liable for any errors in it. If the details of the goods are incomplete, the sender bears the consequences.

The carrier taking over the goods

The carrier's signature means they have taken over the goods and checked what is apparent: the number of packages, their marks and their condition. Hidden defects cannot be detected, so the carrier is not liable for them. When you collect loads from the loading bay, check the boxes before you sign, because your signature binds you to what the note says.

The consignee's signature and the POD

The consignee's signature is the proof of delivery (POD): it proves the goods have been delivered and accepted. Without it, invoicing stays open and you may be left waiting weeks for your money. RAHTIS collects the POD and links it to the invoice automatically, so you know immediately which delivery is complete and which needs a phone call (RAHTIS API documentation).

CMR form boxes 1–10: the parties and the goods

The top half of the form shows who is sending, who is receiving and what was loaded onto the vehicle. These ten boxes determine who can bring a claim and to whom the proof of delivery belongs.

Boxes 1–4: sender, consignee and right of disposal

Box 1 records the sender, i.e. the party that ordered the carriage, and box 2 the consignee's details. Box 3 concerns the right of disposal: normally the consignee may change destinations, but if another address is entered in box 3, the carrier follows that. Box 4 is reserved for the carrier and the vehicle, and most people also enter the trailer here.

Boxes 5–10: details of the goods and attachments

Boxes 5–6 describe the goods: their nature, marks, packing and number of packages. Box 7 is the gross weight and box 8 the volume in cubic metres. Boxes 9–10 record the documents attached to the consignment note, such as customs documents, and the delivery terms. The electronic CMR uses the same boxes, and UNECE maintains an up-to-date list of countries that have adopted the protocol.

Why the top half deserves care: box 3 determines who has the right to change the delivery address during the journey, and without an entry the consignee controls disposal. Boxes 5–8 serve as evidence of quantity and condition at the time of taking over, so a missing number of packages or gross weight shifts the dispute onto the carrier. Boxes 9–10 tie the customs paperwork to the consignment note, and if an attachment isn't mentioned, the carrier bears liability for any missing documents. RAHTIS fills in these boxes directly from the order, and you can explore the API in the Finnish-language RAHTIS API documentation.

CMR form boxes 11–21: carriage, charges and reservations

Boxes 11–21 cover the mechanics of the delivery: route, charges, modes of transport, vehicle and delivery terms. They are short, but this is where most after-the-event disputes arise, because the boxes are often filled in in a hurry at the loading bay.

Boxes 13–15: charges, VAT and delivery terms

Box 13 records the charges paid by the client: carriage, surcharges, duties and other items to be entered in box 20. Enter amounts clearly and show who pays each item. Box 14 is reserved for the VAT breakdown, and box 15 states the delivery term, for example in Incoterms form. If box 13 is left blank but the agreed carriage charge is invoiced later, enter "carriage forward" or similar so that the driver and invoicing have the same information.

Boxes 16–21: vehicles, reservations and time of delivery

Boxes 16–18 concern the vehicle and rolling stock: registration number, trailer and container. Box 19 is reserved for customs instructions, box 20 for reservations and instructions (such as temperature requirements or unloading sequence), and box 21 for the time and date of delivery.

Paper or electronic CMR consignment note?

Both paper and electronic consignment notes are legal. The CMR Convention permits an electronic version, and Traficom (the Finnish Transport and Communications Agency) provides guidance on the use of consignment notes in Finland, so both are valid as proof of delivery.

The legal status of e-CMR

The electronic consignment note (e-CMR) complies with the Additional Protocol to the CMR Convention, and a signature has the same legal effect as on paper. The problem isn't the law but the fact that the loader or consignee may not be on an e-CMR system.

What works in practice in the Nordic countries

For a small fleet, paper still works: the driver gets a signed copy, scans it and sends it to the office. It's slow, and paper gets lost. If your customers are subcontractors found through TIMOCOM or Trans.eu, ask in advance whether they accept e-CMR. RAHTIS collects the POD and consignment note in the same chain for invoicing, but honestly: if the consignee insists on pen and paper, going electronic only gets you halfway.

What happens if the CMR consignment note contains an error or is missing?

A missing or incorrect CMR consignment note does not usually invalidate the contract of carriage, but it shifts the burden of proof onto you: if the goods are damaged, you have to show what you delivered and in what condition. In practice, missing paperwork shows up as late invoices and disputes over whether the goods arrived at all.

The most common mistakes on small hauliers' forms

Box 13 (carrier's reservations) is left blank even though the packaging was poor or the seal was broken. The consignee records the damage in box 18, and without a reservation of your own you carry the risk alone. Other typical ones: the description of the goods is written solely on the sender's say-so, package details are vague ("one load"), and box 16 is left blank, so special transport charges go uninvoiced.

How liability limits relate to insurance

The liability limits of the CMR Convention (e.g. a cap based on gross weight per kilogram) do not replace your insurance, but the insurer assesses the claim precisely on the basis of what is written on the consignment note. A correctly completed form is the cheapest insurance evidence you will ever get.

How does the CMR consignment note fit into day-to-day driving and invoicing?

When you drive and invoice yourself, the consignment note is three documents in one: the contract of carriage, the proof of delivery and an attachment to the invoice. The structure of the standard form supports this: boxes 1–15 say who is carrying what, box 18 the reservations, and the rest the delivery details, as described in the IRU's guidance on the CMR form. Without box 18 and the consignee's signature you have a journey, but no proof of delivery.

POD, rate confirmation and invoice in one chain

In practice, the POD, rate confirmation and invoice form a chain: the rate confirmation shows the price, the consignment note shows what was carried and box 20 the carrier's reservations, and the consignee's signature releases the invoice. When these three documents are together, the paper "who owes what" notebook becomes unnecessary. Clear handwriting pays: an illegible signature or a missing date is the most common reason for late payment.

Subcontracting from a freight exchange: whose CMR?

When you take a load from TIMOCOM, Trans.eu or Teleroute, the client usually provides its own consignment note on which you are entered as the carrier. Check box 16: your company must appear there if you want your own liability and your own invoice for the freight exchange booking. Keep a copy yourself: if the client disputes the delivery, your signature on the consignment note is your only evidence.

FAQ

Can the sender or a broker fill in the CMR consignment note instead of the carrier?

Yes. The sender fills in the key details of the consignment note and is responsible for their accuracy. A broker or client may also provide a ready-made consignment note on which you are entered as the carrier. Your liability is limited to what you sign: check the number of packages, their marks and condition before signing, because your signature binds you to what the note says.

What is box 11 (declarations) on the CMR and when is it mandatory?

Box 11 is reserved for the sender's declarations, i.e. instructions on handling the goods during carriage. It isn't mandatory for every journey, but if the goods need special handling, such as temperature requirements or a specific unloading sequence, the instructions must be on paper. Verbal instructions don't appear on the consignment note, so an unrecorded instruction shifts the risk of a dispute onto you.

How many originals and copies of the CMR form are needed?

The standard form comprises one original for the carrier, one for the consignee and one for the sender, plus copies for other parties. For a small haulier, the most important ones are the copy that travels with you and the proof of delivery signed by the consignee. Keep a copy yourself, because if the client disputes the delivery, the signed consignment note is your only evidence.

Can a CMR consignment note be corrected afterwards if I spot a mistake?

A mistake can be corrected, but the correction must be agreed by all parties and be traceable. A private amendment made in pen without signatures loses its evidential value in a dispute. If you spot the mistake before signing, correct it immediately. If the consignee has already recorded reservations, add your own remark and ask for it to be confirmed.

Is a photographed POD without a signed CMR acceptable for invoicing?

A photo can support an invoice, but it does not replace a signed proof of delivery. Without the consignee's signature, invoicing stays open and the burden of proof shifts to you: you have to show what you delivered and in what condition. Best practice is to scan the signed paper and attach it to the invoice together with the photos.
