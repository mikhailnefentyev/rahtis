  • Track the share of empty running weekly: empty kilometres divided by all kilometres driven.

Reducing empty kilometres with a small vehicle comes down to three things: look for a backload before unloading, combine jobs and customer routes on the same day, and track the share of empty running every week. With a vehicle under 3.5 tonnes, an empty kilometre weighs relatively more than with heavy equipment, because the margin per transport is lower. This guide focuses on van and light goods vehicle work, not on planning heavy combination traffic.

Why do empty kilometres eat a bigger share of the margin with a small vehicle?

In light haulage, empty running is financially more critical than in heavy haulage, because the load, and with it the freight rate, is small, while the vehicle's costs keep running. When a van runs empty, revenue is zero, but fuel, wear and driver time cost almost as much as when it is loaded.

Cost structure: fixed and variable costs for a vehicle under 3.5 tonnes

Fixed costs, such as insurance, vehicle tax, depreciation and lease payments, run every day, whether the vehicle is driven or not. Among variable costs, fuel and driver time weigh heavily with light vehicles, because the margin per hour is small. One empty hour therefore takes a large share of the day's margin. With a heavy combination, an empty kilometre is more expensive, but freight rates are higher and the cost is spread over more tonnes.

Worked example: what an empty day means over a month

If a light vehicle works 22 days a month and one day is spent entirely empty, about 4–5 per cent of working days earn nothing. Two empty days push the share to about nine per cent. Empty running is significant in heavy haulage too: according to Statistics Finland, 23 per cent of the kilometres driven by Finnish-registered lorries were driven empty in 2016 (Statistics Finland, road freight transport). No equivalent statistics are published for vans, so your own tracking is the only reliable figure. Every backload you find goes straight to the margin.

Where can backloads be found for light vehicles?

Backloads for a van can be found in three places: freight exchanges, your own customers and direct cooperation with other hauliers. The largest exchanges, such as TIMOCOM, Teleroute and Trans.eu, mainly serve heavy vehicles, so search for light vehicle loads using filters rather than browsing.

Be findable: have your posting and alerts ready

A backload is often found because the shipper knows your vehicle is free. Keep a vehicle posting ready on the exchange, showing the vehicle, capacity, time available and return area. Set route alerts before you set off, not once you are already driving empty.

How do you plan routes and runs to leave less empty running?

The day's round trip before the single transport

Plan the day as a round trip, not as a string of one-off transports. When you look at the day as a whole first thing in the morning, you can see straight away which run has an empty return and where you should look for a backload.

A light vehicle's cost structure turns this into real money: fuel and wear make up a large part of the cost per kilometre, so an empty kilometre costs almost as much as a loaded one. Because the margin per job is small, a few dozen empty kilometres a day can take a significant share of the day's margin. Work out your own figure: multiply the empty kilometres by your own cost per kilometre and compare the total with the day's margin.

A practical routine: in the morning, write down the starting point, the delivery points and the estimated time the vehicle will be free. Then check whether there is a load in the return direction that fits the schedule and the size of the vehicle. If not, widen the round: a second customer in the same direction may fill the return leg. In RAHTIS, your own jobs, dispatch and backloads from the load board are in the same service, so checking is quicker than jumping between different services. The system does not plan the route for you, however; it makes the round visible.

Early information saves the backload

A backload is often agreed hours before unloading. If you only hear about a load when the vehicle is already leaving empty, the opportunity has gone. Agree with your customers that you will get load details in good time, and allow a 30–60 minute buffer in the day between unloading and the next loading.

What role do local and regional rounds play?

Regular customers as the backbone, the freight exchange to fill the gaps

Regular customers fill the kilometres you cannot get from a freight exchange. When you have a weekly round with a factory, a distribution point or a construction site, you know the start and end points in advance. That makes looking for a backload realistic: you search the exchange for a load that fits your round's route and schedule.

In practice, this works best when fixed rounds cover the core kilometres and exchange loads fill the gaps. When jobs, proof of delivery and invoices are in the same system, the invoice for a regular round customer does not get forgotten in a paper notebook in between exchange loads (RAHTIS).

A familiar area beats nationwide coverage

When you drive in the same area, you know where unloading happens in the afternoons and who has loads on Mondays. Local knowledge cuts empty kilometres more than any tool: you learn customers' unloading times, the congestion and the yards where a phone call will get you a backload.

Nationwide coverage sounds safer, but with a small vehicle it often means driving to unfamiliar places with no return load. Two or three familiar areas where rounds and exchange loads overlap produce less empty running than one-off loads to different parts of Finland.

How do a freight exchange and a transport management system together reduce empty running?

A freight exchange tells you where loads are. A management system tells you where your own vehicles are and when they become free. When both are visible at the same time, the search for a backload starts from the route: you look at where the vehicle has to go anyway, and find a load going there.

RAHTIS does not guarantee loads and does not aggregate loads from other exchanges. It shortens the path from quote to invoice and shows backloads from its own load board, but you find loads and rates from other exchanges in their own services.

Alternatives: TIMOCOM, Teleroute and Trans.eu

TIMOCOM, Teleroute and Trans.eu are each separate freight exchanges, and their light vehicle offering and prices should be checked directly with each service. If you use several exchanges in parallel, work out how much time the separate logins take, and focus on the one that actually has loads for your route.

What mistakes do small-vehicle operators most often make in load planning?

Looking for a backload only once the vehicle is already empty

The most common mistake is looking for a backload only once the delivery has been made and the vehicle is standing idle. By then you have minutes, and the options are to take any load at all or drive home empty. A better way is to look for a backload before you commit to the outbound transport. If no return load is in sight, price the outbound transport to cover both directions.

The second pitfall is keeping the books in a paper notebook: when the information about who owes what lives in your head, the share of empty running is not visible anywhere and you cannot fix it.

Too wide a search area and poor pricing

Many search for loads across the whole country and drive 150 kilometres empty to chase a "cheap" job. With a light vehicle, the freight rate also has to cover the empty run to collection and the empty return, or the kilometres eat the margin.

To be honest: RAHTIS does not automatically calculate the margin per route or the share of empty running. How do you track the share of empty running in practice?

A simple measure: empty kilometres / all kilometres

The measure is a single division: kilometres driven empty divided by all kilometres driven. At the end of each day, write down how many kilometres you drove loaded and how many empty. Paper or a spreadsheet is fine, as long as you record it every day.

If the empty share is over a third, more than one kilometre in three is driven without revenue. The figure tells you whether to look harder for backloads or to price the empty return into the outbound job.

Monthly review and what to do with the figures

Once a month, look at three figures: the share of empty running, the route or customer that caused the most empty running, and the load you missed out on. With one vehicle, 50 empty kilometres a day over 22 working days a month comes to about 1,100 kilometres for which you pay fuel and wear with no revenue.

Use the figures to make decisions: if a particular customer consistently leaves you with an empty return, build it into the rate or drop the customer. The measure does not replace planning, but it shows you where planning should start.

FAQ

How high a share of empty running is still profitable with a small vehicle?

There is no universal limit, because it depends on freight rates and your own costs. As a point of comparison: according to Statistics Finland, 23 per cent of lorry kilometres were driven empty in 2016. If your own share is well over a third, more than one kilometre in three earns nothing, and you should look at how you find backloads or how you price. Track the share weekly and make decisions based on the monthly review.

Are there loads on freight exchanges for vehicles under 3.5 tonnes?

Yes, although the largest exchanges, such as TIMOCOM, Teleroute and Trans.eu, mainly serve heavy vehicles. Search using filters for weight, vehicle type and timing. Load availability varies by region, so first count how many suitable loads appear on your route each week before committing to one exchange.

How do I price the outbound transport if no backload can be found?

If no return load is found before you commit, price the outbound transport to cover both directions. With a light vehicle, the margin per job is small, so an empty return can take a large share of the day's margin. If a particular customer consistently leaves you with an empty return, build it into the rate or drop the customer.

Do I need separate routing software, or is a map app enough?

A map app is enough for driving, but it does not show your jobs, available loads or the share of empty running. What matters is not routing but seeing your own jobs and the available loads in good time. Without that, you look for a backload too late.
