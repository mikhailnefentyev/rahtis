A dat load board for box trucks only works if you configure it for your equipment: set equipment type to van or straight truck, filter deadhead to 150 miles or less, and read rate view as a per-mile range, not an average. This guide walks through those exact settings, then weighs DAT against free load boards so a one-to-twelve truck operation can decide if the subscription pays for itself.

How do you filter DAT load board searches for box truck equipment types?

Equipment type: van vs straight truck vs hotshot

DAT's search settings let you pick equipment types before anything else, and the DAT load board documentation covers the filter options in its help centre. For a box truck, start with "Van" if you run a straight box with a liftgate, and add "Straight Truck" as a second equipment type so you see both postings. Skip "Hotshot" unless you actually tow; those loads assume a gooseneck and a pickup with a trailer, and brokers posting there expect different weight ceilings.

Radius and lane filters that match a box truck's range

The filter that actually saves you time is the origin radius, because a box truck lives on short regional runs, not cross-country lanes. Set your origin radius to 100-150 miles and your destination radius wider, around 200-300 miles, which matches a same-day or next-day round trip without a forced overnight. Leave the length and weight fields alone unless a shipper specifies, since DAT posts dimensions per load rather than per equipment filter, and blank beats wrong. If you chase return loads to cut empty running, save the search once and let it persist; the same saved-lane logic applies whether you work DAT or a Nordic rahtipörssi like RAHTIS, where publishing a keikka once replaces re-entering it into every load board you are logged into.

What does DAT's rate view show for box truck lanes and how do you read it?

DAT's rate view pulls historical rates for a lane and equipment type, so you can see what straight trucks actually got paid on that lane before you spend a minute on the phone.

Spot rate vs contract rate on straight-truck lanes

Spot rates are one-off loads priced at the moment; contract rates are committed lanes at a fixed rate. On box truck lanes the gap matters less than on van lanes, but always check which one the rate view is showing. A posted load above the spot average is worth the call; one below it usually means the broker is fishing.

Reading per-mile averages against your cost per km

Convert the lane's per-mile average to per-km and hold it against your real cost per km: fuel, driver, insurance, depreciation. If a Helsinki–Tampere keikka pays under your cost line, no volume makes it worth running empty back. Rate view tells you the market price; only your own numbers tell you if it's a good job.

Which DAT workflow steps take a box truck load from search to booked?

From search to call: what to have ready

Filter to a straight truck or box truck equipment type, set your deadhead radius, and save the lane search so you can rerun it each morning. Before you dial, know three numbers: your rate per mile floor, what DAT's Rate View shows for the lane's recent spot rates, and whether the shipper will tarp or hand-load. Brokers ask for your MC number, insurance certificate, and references on the first call, so keep those in one file.

Booking, confirmation and the paperwork trail

Once you agree on a rate, get the rate confirmation in writing before you dispatch the truck, and check that it matches what you negotiated, including detention and lumper terms. The broker then sends the carrier packet; you sign, send insurance and W-9 back, and the load is booked. From there the trail is the signed rate con, the CMR or bill of lading at pickup, and the signed POD, which triggers your invoice. If you run Nordic freight, a combined TMS and freight exchange like RAHTIS collapses that paperwork into one flow: the job, the rahtikirja and the invoice follow each other instead of living in a notebook and three inboxes.

How do free load boards for box trucks compare on load volume and booking speed?

What free load boards give you and what they cost you in time

Free boards cost nothing up front and charge you in hours. You refresh manually, call brokers to ask rates because no rate data is shown, and re-enter the same load details into your spreadsheet, notebook and paper CMR. For one truck with steady direct clients, that trade can be fine. For a 4-truck outfit where you are dispatcher, accountant and safety guy at once, the refresh-and-call loop is where the day disappears.

DAT flips the trade: you pay a subscription and get rate view, saved lane filters and alerts that push loads to you, so booking speed comes from fewer manual checks rather than more phone calls.

Is DAT worth the subscription for a single box truck or pickup operation?

Honest answer: probably not, unless you cross borders regularly. DAT's subscription tiers are priced for brokers and carriers chasing volume, and its rate data is strongest on North American lanes (DAT freight rates and subscription overview). For a one-truck operator in Finland, the break-even math is simple: if the subscription plus your time filtering loads costs more than the margin on one extra return load per month, it loses.

Break-even logic for a one-truck operation

Count what a paid board must produce: one booked return load per month that you would not have found otherwise. If you already have two or three regular clients, a notebook dispatch, and a freight exchange login, the marginal loads a US-focused board surfaces rarely justify the per-user cost of a paid board.

Regional alternative: Nordic and Baltic freight exchanges

Exchanges like Trans.eu or TIMOCOM cover the lanes you actually drive. A combined tool such as RAHTIS puts its own load board and dispatch in one place, so one login replaces the board-plus-TMS stack.

What load types should box truck operators avoid on DAT?

Overweight and trailer-shaped freight

DAT lets brokers post under any equipment type, so check the weight and pallet count before you call. A "partial" posting with 8 pallets or 15,000 lbs is a trailer load wearing a box truck label. If the commodity is dense, ask: steel, liquids, and packaged flooring regularly blow past straight-truck payload limits. Skip lanes where the rate only works with a 53-foot trailer's volume.

Hidden handling requirements: liftgate, inside delivery, appointment freight

The details that eat your margin live in the remarks, not the rate. A load with no liftgate flag that turns out to need one, or an inside delivery at a mall dock, can add an unpaid hour. Appointment-only freight is worse for a solo dispatcher: you hold the keikka slot while your options for a return load shrink. Read the full notes before booking, and price residential or jobsite deliveries as if the access will be bad.

How do you set up DAT alerts so box truck loads near your route reach you first?

Watch lanes and alert frequency

In DAT, a watch lane is a saved lane plus equipment filter. Set equipment to Straight Truck or Van, define origin and destination radius (50 miles around your usual route works for pickups), then choose push alerts so the load arrives on your phone the moment it posts. Speed matters: on lanes with heavy freight exchange volume, like the corridors covered by TIMOCOM's European freight exchange, the first carrier to call often takes the load.

Pairing alerts with your empty-running pattern

Set alerts on the return leg of your regular keikka before you leave the yard. If you know Tuesday ends in Turku, a watch lane from Turku north catches return loads while you are still driving, so the tyhjäajo home becomes a paid backhaul instead of dead miles.

FAQ

Can you use DAT load board for pickup trucks and hotshot loads?

Yes, but with caveats. DAT posts hotshot loads under the Hotshot equipment type, and those assume a pickup towing a gooseneck, not a cargo bed alone. Pickup operators without a trailer will find few matching postings, and box truck operators should skip Hotshot entirely since brokers there expect different weight ceilings and trailer-based pricing.

How many box truck loads are posted on DAT per day?

DAT does not publish a daily count broken down by equipment type, so no exact figure exists. What is known is that DAT's overall North American load volume is the largest among paid boards, and straight truck and van postings are a subset of that. Expect thin volume on narrow regional lanes and denser volume around major metro corridors.

Is a freight exchange like Trans.eu or Teleroute a better fit than DAT for Nordic box truck freight?

Usually yes. Trans.eu and TIMOCOM cover the lanes a Nordic box truck actually drives, with shippers and carriers already on them. DAT's rate data and volume are strongest on North American lanes, so its subscription rarely pays off for regional Finnish or Baltic work. A combined exchange and dispatch tool suits that market even better.
