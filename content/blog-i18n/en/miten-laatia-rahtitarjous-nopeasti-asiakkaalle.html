How do you put together a haulage quote for a customer quickly?

In five minutes, if the information is to hand and you know the structure by heart: origin and destination, type of load, weight and volume, schedule, price and payment terms. Write these seven lines in the same order every time and the quote takes shape during the call rather than afterwards in a notebook.

To calculate the price, use the basis for calculating haulage rates and cost recovery published by SKAL (the Finnish Transport and Logistics association), which hauliers in Finland rely on. Once the basis is clear, the quote can be justified straight away. In RAHTIS (rahtis.eu/fi) the same information pre-fills the quote form.

Speed comes from repetition. If you always use the same template, nobody needs to do creative writing in the middle of a busy call. Start the call by asking about the route and the load, after which the price is the only open variable. Enter the answers straight into the ready-made fields and send the message as soon as the call ends.

What information must a haulage quote contain?

Company details and the customer

Start the quote with your company details: name, Business ID, address and a contact person with phone number. Do the same for the customer, so that the documents later match the consignment note and the invoice. Without a Business ID the customer cannot check who they are handing their load to.

A missing Business ID causes invoicing delays and possible mix-ups if there are several companies with the same name. Make sure the contact person is the one who actually makes the decisions, not someone who just passes messages on. This shortens the approval process considerably.

Route, vehicle type and load details

This is the heart of the quote. Write down the details that determine the price and prevent misunderstandings about the load:

Origin and destinationVantaa → TampereDistance determines the price and the empty running

If the load needs a specialist vehicle or ADR certification, say so plainly in the quote. Fewer questions later.

Giving the load dimensions in metres and kilograms is more precise than a pallet count alone. For example, "8 pallets, 120×80×200 cm, 6,000 kg in total" gives the driver confidence that the load will fit and stay within the weight limits. If you leave out the dimensions, the customer may assume standard-size pallets when the load is in fact out of gauge.

Validity period and payment terms

Without a validity period the quote stays open forever, and the price of fuel may change before the customer replies. State, for example, "valid for 7 days", or tie the price to a specific departure date. Payment terms belong in the quote from the start: 14 days net is common, and if you need 30 days, agree it before the job is done. The RAHTIS API documentation (https://www.rahtis.eu/fi/api-docs) shows how these fields carry over from the quote to the consignment note and the invoice without retyping.

The validity period protects you against swings in market prices, especially fuel. If you tie the price to a specific departure date, the customer understands that delays may lead to a new quote. Stating the payment terms in the quote prevents surprises at invoicing and gives the customer a chance to comment on the terms before the job starts.

How do you price a haulage job in minutes?

Fixed price or hourly rate

Choose the model according to the nature of the job. A fixed price suits regular runs where the route and the type of work are known in advance. An hourly rate makes sense when the work includes unloading, waiting or several drops that you cannot estimate beforehand.

The advantage of a fixed price is predictability for the customer: they know the exact sum in advance and need not fear unexpected costs. The advantage of an hourly rate is on your side: if unloading drags on or the customer is late, you invoice for the work done. Many hauliers use a hybrid: a fixed price for the basic job and an hourly rate for extra work, such as waiting beyond the agreed time.

Empty running and return loads

Let us work through an example you can apply directly to your own jobs. Start from a rate per kilometre that covers fuel, the driver's wages, insurance and vehicle wear, and add your margin. If the customer's job is 300 km but there is no confirmed return load for the journey home, price it on 600 kilometres, i.e. both directions. This is fair, because an empty kilometre costs you almost as much as a loaded one.

Tell the customer plainly that the price includes the empty leg; that way the justification is ready and neither side has to guess. If you get a return load from the freight exchange or from your own customers, you can take the empty running out and offer a more competitive price. In RAHTIS you publish your empty legs at https://www.rahtis.eu/et/apply, so a return load can cover part of the journey before the quote goes out.

A concrete example: the rate is €1.20 per kilometre and the margin is 15 per cent. A one-way 300 km job with no return load costs 600 km × €1.20 × 1.15 = €828. If you find a return load, the price drops to 300 km × €1.20 × 1.15 = €414. That €414 difference is your room to negotiate while still keeping your margin.

Which extra costs to include

Include a waiting time agreement (e.g. first hour free, hourly rate after that), quality-related charges, weather delays and handling of the CMR consignment note. Traficom (the Finnish Transport and Communications Agency) provides guidance on consignment note and CMR requirements in road transport (Traficom), so make sure your extra charges also cover the documentation, whether electronic or on paper.

Forgetting extra costs is the most common reason a profitable job turns into a loss-maker. For example, occasional waiting at the delivery point can stretch to hours, and if you have not agreed compensation, you are doing it in your own time. Always state the waiting terms in the quote: the first hour is included in the price, after that €45–60 per hour.

How do you justify the price to the customer without haggling?

A good quote justifies the price with the route, vehicle type and schedule, not with discounts. When the quote shows the kilometres, the nature of the route (for example a loose trailer with no return load), the type of vehicle needed and the collection and delivery times, the customer can see where the money goes. Empty running and the lack of a return route are real costs, and showing them openly stops most price haggling.

Also state what is included: the consignment note (CMR), waiting time terms and payment terms. When everything is in writing, the negotiation moves from details to the overall price, which you have already justified.

The same principle works for your own customers and for jobs from the freight exchange. Once a quote has been built properly, the next one goes out as a copy. In RAHTIS you can build a reusable quote template for your own routes and sign up at https://www.rahtis.eu/fi/apply, from a single lorry upwards.

Justification matters even more when you are competing with larger operators. Big companies can offer a low rate per kilometre, but they carry overheads and delays. Your advantage is speed and flexibility. When you base the price realistically on costs and show the calculation openly, a small haulier can win a tender against a bigger operator, because the customer understands how the price is made up and trusts it.

How do you send the quote and follow up the reply?

Email works for regular customers, but the message easily gets lost in the customer's inbox. Freight exchange messaging is the fastest way to reach those looking for a loose trailer move: Teleroute, TIMOCOM and Trans.eu, for example, handle the quote and the reply in the same system, so you have a written record of the price and terms (how a freight exchange works).

Choose the channel to suit the job: email for a regular customer, the freight exchange for an empty leg, RAHTIS when you want everything in one place. In RAHTIS you publish the job once, carriers reply directly and the transport progresses step by step in the same view: https://www.rahtis.eu/lt/apply.

Follow-up is the part that a notebook-and-spreadsheet setup loses. Set a reminder for 24 hours later: if there is no reply, call or send a follow-up message. Log every quote you send and its status (pending, rejected, accepted), so you can see each week which customers and which routes actually pay.

Following up quotes is a numbers game. If you send ten quotes a week and three are left hanging without a reply, you are losing potential turnover. A simple spreadsheet with columns for customer, route, price, date sent and status is enough to get started. When you see, for example, that 80 per cent of your quotes on the Helsinki–Turku route are accepted, you can focus more on it.

How does a freight exchange speed up the quoting process?

What freight exchanges offer for quoting

A freight exchange gives you ready-made load details that you would otherwise collect by phone and write in a notebook: origin and destination, date, trailer type, weight and load description. When a customer asks for a quote, you check the rate for a comparable route on the exchange and add your own margin on top. Transportial, Teleroute, TIMOCOM, Trans.eu and Maxmove all work on the same principle here: they show the market rate on the route, and you decide whether to go below or above it.

The exchange also shortens negotiation. When the customer sees that there is demand on the route, your price is easier to accept without a long tussle.

Seeing the market rate gives you a realistic idea of the prices other operators are working at. If your own price is clearly above the market, you can either lower it or justify the difference: faster delivery, fewer delays or better customer service. If your price is below the market, you can raise your margin without losing the customer.

What are the most common mistakes in a haulage quote and how do you avoid them?

The first mistake is a missing validity period. Without a date the quote lives forever, and the customer may come back three weeks later demanding the same price even though fuel prices have changed. Always add a line saying "quote valid for 7 days" or similar.

The second mistake is a vague vehicle type. "Lorry" does not tell anyone whether the job suits a drawbar trailer, a swap body or a van. If the customer assumes something else and you send the wrong vehicle, the empty running is at your expense. State the type, volume and payload.

The third mistake is forgetting the payment terms. An agreement noted in a paper notebook without terms means that "who owes what" is left to your memory. State the payment period, for example 14 days net, and whether you charge interest on late payment.

These three lines take a minute but save negotiation and debt-chasing later.

The fourth mistake is missing detail in the load description. "Pallets" is not enough, as pallets can come in different sizes and contain different goods. State the number of pallets, the dimensions and the weight, so the driver knows what they are collecting and what space the load will fit in. The fifth mistake is forgetting additional services: if the customer expects unloading and you have priced only the transport, the job becomes a loss-maker.

FAQ

Can I send a haulage quote directly to a freight exchange without a customer of my own?

Yes, you can. Freight exchanges such as Teleroute, TIMOCOM and Trans.eu list loose trailer jobs that you can respond to directly in the system without an existing customer relationship. The exchange gives you the details ready-made: route, date, vehicle type, weight and load description. You add your own margin and submit the quote. This works particularly well for filling empty legs.

What should I do if the customer asks for a discount on the quote?

If the quote justifies the price with the route, vehicle type, kilometres and schedule, most price haggling stops of its own accord. When the customer sees where the money goes, the negotiation moves to the overall price. If a discount is still requested, see whether a return load is available: it lets you take out the empty running, making the price genuinely more competitive without the deal becoming a loss.

How long is a haulage quote valid by default?

There should be no default: always state the validity period, for example seven days. Without a date the quote remains open forever, and the customer may come back weeks later demanding the same price even though fuel has gone up. Alternatively, you can tie the price to a specific departure date, so the schedule sets the validity for you.

Do I need a separate TMS if I only send a few quotes a week?

Not necessarily. A notebook and a spreadsheet work until the quote, the consignment note and the invoice live in different places and the data has to be moved by hand.
