Order tracking means following each order as the transport moves through clear statuses: collection, loading, swap, delivery and confirmation of the signed consignment note (POD). This guide shows how to set up the workflow so the customer can see the situation themselves and you save on phone calls, notes and spreadsheets, even if you run loose trailers or swap containers.

What does order tracking mean in practice?

Order tracking means tracking per order: a single consignment is followed step by step from collection to delivery, rather than the lorry's position on a map. In other words, it is about transport events, such as loading, unloading and the consignment note coming back, not vehicle location data.

The distinction matters because many small hauliers currently track only their own lorry or phone the customer to ask about the load. With order tracking, the customer can see what stage their particular job has reached, and the driver marks the stages along the way. Behind the process is the consignment note, which in international transport is the CMR: it is not the contract itself but evidence of the contract of carriage, its terms and the receipt of the goods (Articles 4 and 9 of the CMR Convention), and liability is determined by the Convention.

In practice order tracking can work without a separate GPS system: the driver updates the status, and a system such as RAHTIS (https://www.rahtis.eu/) shows the order's progress all the way to the customer.

How does order tracking differ from real-time vehicle tracking?

Order tracking means status-based tracking: the transport progresses stage by stage from collection to delivery, and the customer can see where their own consignment is. GPS-based vehicle tracking, on the other hand, follows the lorry, not the load. The same lorry may carry three different orders in a day, in which case vehicle tracking tells you where the lorry is, but not whose consignment is on board.

For a small firm, status tracking is often enough, because it tells the customer exactly what they ask on the phone: has the load been collected, is it on its way and has it been delivered. A GPS system adds devices, monthly fees and maintenance, the benefit of which is small for a small fleet.

When GPS tracking is still needed

GPS tracking is worthwhile if your customer requires real-time lorry location written into the contract, if you run vehicle operating hours or service intervals from the system, or if your fleet is spread across several drivers and you want a fleet manager's view. Theft risk and mandatory monitoring of drivers' working time can also justify a device. In other cases, status tracking meets the customer's needs more cheaply and with less hassle.

What information should you show the customer in order tracking?

The customer needs five things: the current status (collected, in transit, delivered), the estimated schedule, exceptions such as a delay or rerouting, the consignment note number and the POD once the signed delivery note has reached the system. This is enough to eliminate almost every customer service call: when the customer can see the status themselves, they do not ring to ask "where's the lorry?".

Avoid showing the driver's personal details, precise real-time coordinates, freight rates or other customers on the same route. When the vehicle is driven by an identifiable driver, its location data is that driver's personal data, so passing it on is restricted by the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, Regulation (EU) 2016/679).

In practice you share a link with the customer that shows only that order's details. In RAHTIS this works by the order progressing from collection to delivery and the customer sees the stages with time stamps via a tracking link, without logging in; the API documentation describes how status data can be shared with the customer's own systems (https://www.rahtis.eu/en/api-docs). An honest limitation: RAHTIS does not show a map accurate to the minute – tracking is based on status updates, which is enough for most loose trailer and job-by-job transport.

How are one-off jobs, such as loose trailer and container transport, tracked stage by stage?

Order tracking for loose trailer and container transport is built on four stages: publication, collection, swap and delivery. Each stage is updated as soon as the driver or the transport planner records the event, so the customer can see the situation without phone calls.

Stage 1: publishing and accepting the task

The job is published once, either on the freight exchange or directly to the customer: collection point, destination, vehicle requirement and schedule. Once accepted, the task gets its own reference, around which all later information builds up. A task published in RAHTIS goes straight into your own workflow, so there is no need for separate exchange records or copying into a spreadsheet.

Stage 2: collection and load confirmation

The driver records arrival at the collection point, and loading is confirmed either in the app or with the consignment note details. At this stage it is worth recording the container or trailer number, because it is the only sure way to identify the right unit when the same equipment is used on several jobs. An incorrect container number is the most common reason tracking shows the details of the wrong unit.

Stage 3: swaps and intermediate statuses

In container and loose trailer traffic the load often changes vehicle mid-journey: at a port, a terminal or a partner's yard. The swap is recorded as a separate event, so the time stamps are kept and the customer can see what stage the load is at even when the vehicle changes. Without recording these intermediate stages, tracking breaks off at exactly the points where customers ask most often.

Stage 4: delivery and POD

Delivery is confirmed with a POD: a receipt, signature or photo is attached directly to the task. RAHTIS combines the consignment note, POD and payment tracking for the invoice in the same record: once you mark the invoice on the job, RAHTIS tracks the due date and reminds the customer about an unpaid invoice. Through the API (https://www.rahtis.eu/fi/api-docs) the status can also be passed on to the customer's own system.

How is tracking information shared with the customer automatically?

There are three practical ways: a shared tracking link, email notifications when the status changes and API integration. For most firms with 1–12 vehicles, the first two are enough.

Shared link vs. email notifications

A shared link is the simplest way: once the order is in RAHTIS, you send the customer a link through which they can see the status from collection to delivery. You do not call, the customer does not call you, and you can send the link once and forget about it. Email notifications work when the customer cannot be bothered to click anything: the system sends a message automatically when the status changes, for example "collected" or "delivered".

A shared link has one honest limitation: the customer sees the status only when you update it. If you forget to mark the collection as done, the customer sees out-of-date information. That is why the status is best updated on the phone while on the road, not in a notebook at home in the evening.

API integration

API integration means tracking data goes straight into the customer's own system without emails or links. It is mainly relevant when your customer is large and needs the data in its own system. RAHTIS has an open, free REST API for shippers, with webhooks that report changes in order status (for example order.taken, order.started and order.closed); the documentation is at /api-docs. Even so, an integration is rarely the first step for a small haulier, so it is worth coming back to it when a larger customer asks for it.

Freight exchanges such as Teleroute and Trans.eu are first and foremost places where loads and capacity meet, and their tracking features vary depending on the service and the tools the customer uses. That is why final order tracking and customer communication are best kept in your own systems, not behind an exchange login.

What are the most common tracking problems and how are they solved?

The biggest problem is the driver not updating the status. The solution is to make updating so easy that it needs no call to the transport planner: a single tap on the mobile says that the lorry is at the collection point, loaded or unloaded. When the status is updated from the road, dispatch can see the situation without notes.

The second problem is an exception that goes unreported: a delay, damage or a wrong address. When the order is tracked stage by stage from collection to delivery, the exception is recorded on the same order with a time stamp, so you can tell the customer straight away rather than when they ring.

The third classic is the paper consignment note going missing. The solution is to capture the consignment note and POD digitally the moment delivery is completed and attach them to the order. RAHTIS itself describes how the driver receives the job and status updates directly on their phone and the documents are saved in the system (source).

The fourth problem is the customer ringing with the question "where's the lorry?". When the order status and documents can be shared as a link, you can answer by looking at your own screen. To be honest: if the driver still does not update the status, no system will fix that, so learning the routine is part of getting started.

How do you introduce order tracking as part of transport management?

The first week in practice

Day one: add your own vehicles and drivers to RAHTIS. Day two: take one real job from the freight exchange and run it through the system from collection to POD, without duplicating it on paper. Day three: send the customer the first tracking link and ask whether it looks like what they need. Rest of the week: move the remaining active jobs into the system and deliberately put the notebook aside. If something breaks, fix it in the system rather than going back to the old way.

In a small firm the responsibilities are clear: whoever accepts the job marks its status. The driver updates collection and delivery; you confirm the consignment note and invoicing. One rule is enough: the status changes only in the system, not by agreeing over the phone to do it "later".

Where RAHTIS is enough and where it is not

RAHTIS is enough for what most firms with 1–12 vehicles need order tracking for: the order is tracked stage by stage from collection to delivery, the customer sees the status of their own job without calling, and filling empty legs happens in the same place as dispatch. It does not replace a GPS tracker fitted to the vehicle, so real-time map position and vehicle data come from elsewhere if you want them. Nor is it a full accounting program, although payment tracking for invoices is covered. An honest test: if your customers demand minute-by-minute real-time location or ERP integrations, you need a bigger system alongside it. If what you need is for jobs to progress visibly without phone calls, this is enough.

FAQ

How much does order tracking cost a small haulage firm?

The cost depends on the system, but order-based tracking is typically much cheaper than a GPS solution fitted to the vehicle, because it needs no devices, installation or maintenance. In RAHTIS, tracking is part of the order workflow, so there is no separate tracking charge. When comparing, also count the time saved: fewer calls and notes mean fewer working hours.

Can order tracking be done without a driver app?

Yes, but it shifts the work to the transport planner. Someone has to update the statuses, and if the driver does not do it from their phone, you mark collections, swaps and deliveries yourself based on reports from the road. This works with a small number of jobs, but some of the benefit is lost: statuses lag and the customer's visibility suffers. In practice an app for the driver is the simplest way to keep the information up to date.

Does order tracking also work for one-off jobs taken from a freight exchange?

Yes, and that is where it is most useful. A job taken from a freight exchange gets its own reference, around which collection, load confirmation, any swap and delivery with POD build up. A task published in RAHTIS goes straight into your own workflow without separate records or copying into a spreadsheet. The customer sees the stages of their own consignment even if the same lorry carries several different loads in the course of the day.

How are the POD and consignment note made part of tracking?

The delivery stage is confirmed with a POD: a receipt, signature or photo is attached directly to the order the moment the delivery is completed. That way the paper consignment note does not go missing, and the consignment note, POD and payment tracking for the invoice stay in the same record. Payment reminders go out automatically if an invoice you have marked falls overdue, and the documents can be shared with the customer as a link without chasing email attachments afterwards.

Does order tracking need a separate GPS tracker in the vehicle?

No. Order tracking is based on stage updates recorded by the driver or transport planner along the way, so nothing is fitted to the vehicle. A GPS unit is only worthwhile if your customers require real-time lorry location in the contract, you need maintenance or working time data, or your fleet is so spread out that a fleet manager's view is essential. For most firms with 1–12 vehicles, status information is enough for their customers.
